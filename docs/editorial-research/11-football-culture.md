# Football Culture: The Language Kits Belong To

**Research dossier — MOMUTO editorial research, Area 11. Compiled August 2026.**

Football kits are not standalone products; they are artefacts inside a dense cultural language that fans, ultras, musicians, collectors and designers have been writing for over a century. This dossier maps six regions of that language: the organised visual culture of ultras (tifos, colour codes, territorial graffiti); the origin stories that turned arbitrary colour choices into sacred regional identity; football's fifty-year feedback loop with music, from terrace casuals through Britpop to grime and French street-football rap; the blokecore phenomenon and the vintage-shirt economy it supercharged (Classic Football Shirts as the hard data point); the modern ritual system around kit releases — leaks, drops, queues and price revolts; and the counterintuitive finding that tiny clubs (Venezia, Clapton CFC, Dulwich Hamlet) now routinely out-design the giants because meaning, not budget, is the scarce resource. The through-line for MOMUTO: kit design is read by its audience as identity work, and the cultural rules for reading it were written from below — in curvas, cages, terraces and market stalls — long before brands noticed.

## 1. The ultras' visual language: tifos, colour codes and painted territory

**Core insight:** Ultras culture is the largest sustained amateur graphic-design movement in the world — a coherent visual language (choreographed tifos, strict colour codes, territorial graffiti) built by unpaid fans to express identity, history and resistance, decades before clubs employed brand designers.

**Supporting evidence:**
- Tifo culture originated in Western Europe in the 1960s–70s; AC Milan's Curva Sud is credited with the first large-scale tifo banner in 1984 — black and red stripes reading "FORZA VECCHIO CUORE ROSSONERO" ("Come on old red-and-black heart") — an early example of fans rendering club colours as monumental typography. (Tipstering, "The Art of the Tifo: Supporter Choreography Through the Decades" — https://tipstering.com/the-art-of-the-tifo-supporter-choreography-through-the-decades/)
- An Erasmus University Rotterdam thesis, "Football's Visual Language: Tifos as Expressions of Club Identity," analyses tifos as expressions of "imagined communities" — shared histories, values and territorial claims — with ultras integrating historical references as "visual monuments" rooted in localism and resistance. (Erasmus University thesis repository — https://thesis.eur.nl/pub/76785/36527.pdf)
- Academic treatment is expanding: *The Art of Tifo* (Kassing & Karsay, Peter Lang) is a book-length study of choreography as fandom practice. (Peter Lang — https://www.peterlang.com/document/1162452; review at idrottsforum.org — https://idrottsforum.org/karsar_kassing-mean221214/)
- Tifos double as protest media — from their earliest Italian days they have called out corruption, discrimination and club management, making the stand a site of visual dissent, not just celebration. (Sofascore, "Tifo Culture in Football: The Art of Fan Displays" — https://www.sofascore.com/news/tifo-culture-the-art-of-football-fan-displays)
- The language extends off the terraces onto city walls: in Belgrade, the Dorćol district is covered in Partizan murals while Neimar belongs to Red Star; Berlin's club crews use tags, stencils, murals and painted electric boxes to claim neighbourhoods. (UP Magazine, "Berlin, Football, and Graffiti" — https://upmag.com/berlin-football-and-graffiti/)
- A 2026 peer-reviewed study of ultras graffiti in Tetouan, Morocco (International Review for the Sociology of Sport) reads fan graffiti as political and social discourse — "visual testimony to the frustrations experienced by marginalized young men." (SAGE Journals — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10126902251415037)
- The photobook *The Chosen Few: Aesthetics and Ideology in Football-Fan Graffiti and Street Art* documents this as a global aesthetic system. (DoppelHouse Press — https://doppelhouse.com/the-chosen-few/)

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** Ultras culture proves that the strongest football visual identities are made by participants, not commissioned from above. A Sunday-league club choosing colours, a badge and a banner is doing — at small scale — exactly what the Curva Sud did in 1984. Grassroots clubs don't need brand budgets; they need the same raw materials ultras use: a colour code, a story, and repetition.

**Article angles:**
1. "The biggest design studio in the world has no name on the door" — tifo-making as collective amateur graphic design: how a curva plans, funds, paints and executes a 40-metre artwork seen once.
2. "Colour code as constitution" — why ultras police colour usage more strictly than any brand-guidelines PDF, and what designers can learn from that discipline.
3. "The city as a shirt" — Belgrade and Berlin's painted neighbourhoods: what happens when club colours leave the stadium and claim streets.
4. "Protest in club colours" — tifos as dissent media, and the tension when the same fans who make a club's imagery turn it against the boardroom.

**Commercial bridge:** Design Culture (primary), Football Reality (grassroots groups building matchday identity with banners and colour).

## 2. Why colours mean things: accidents that became sacred

**Core insight:** Most club colours began as accidents, borrowings or laundry problems — but a century of repetition welded them to place, so that today a colour change reads as identity theft; colours acquire meaning through use, not through design intent.

**Supporting evidence:**
- Juventus's black and white came from Nottingham: around 1903 the club's pink shirts kept fading in the wash, so English player John Savage had a friend connected to Notts County ship a set of the Magpies' black-and-white striped shirts to Turin — an emergency fix that became one of world sport's most valuable identities. (LeftLion, "Tom Gordon Savage, the Nottingham Man Responsible for Juventus' Iconic Stripes," 2021 — https://leftlion.co.uk/features/2021/09/tom-gordon-savage-notts-county-juventus/; Goal.com, "Why do Juventus play in black and white?" — https://www.goal.com/en/news/why-do-juventus-play-in-black-and-white/szdt01w6llqh1k5ni7lycylqb)
- Juventus's original pink itself was improvised: short of money, the founding Canfari brothers had their mother sew kits from pink and white percale. (BeSoccer — https://www.besoccer.com/new/the-english-side-that-gave-juventus-their-famous-black-and-white-stripes-317457)
- Early clubs mostly needed "some kind of visual identifier" so players could recognise teammates; founders often chose colours "without necessarily putting huge amounts of thought into the decision" — meaning accrued later. (These Football Times, "The football kit family tree: the stories behind clubs' famous colours," 2019 — https://thesefootballtimes.co/2019/09/16/the-football-kit-family-tree-the-stories-behind-clubs-famous-colours/)
- Where colours were chosen deliberately, they encode region: Real Sociedad's blue and white stripes come from the flag of San Sebastián; Hajduk Split's colours represent Dalmatia. Academic work in human geography treats football support as a primary vehicle for "rethinking regional identity" — commitment to place as a central force in football culture. (Human Geographies journal — https://www.humangeographies.org.ro/articles/182/a1822.pdf; The Football Faithful — https://thefootballfaithful.com/how-some-of-footballs-biggest-clubs-got-their-colours/)
- Colour psychology readings circulate in fan culture itself (red = aggression and imposition, blue = tradition and calm, green = local identity and turf), showing fans actively theorise their own colours. (nss sports, "Why are colours so important in football?" — https://www.nss-sports.com/en/lifestyle/41225/colours-football-jerseys; EZ Sports Lab — https://ezsportslab.com/en/blogs/news/significado-colores-camisetas)

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** A new grassroots club choosing colours is making its single most durable design decision — the one thing that will outlive every crest redesign, sponsor and kit cycle. The history says: the choice can be almost arbitrary, but it must then be defended and repeated. Meaning is earned through seasons, not selected from a palette.

**Article angles:**
1. "The laundry accident worth billions" — the full Notts County–Juventus story as a parable of how identity actually forms (contingency first, mythology after).
2. "Pick a colour, keep it forever" — a practical-philosophical guide for new clubs: what a century of colour history says about choosing well.
3. "The flag on your chest" — clubs whose colours are literally their city's flag (San Sebastián, Dalmatia) and what it means to wear a region.
4. "Why recolouring a crest feels like vandalism" — the psychology of colour loyalty, and famous fan revolts against colour changes.

**Commercial bridge:** Design Culture (primary), Ready to Play Thinking (colour choice as the founding design decision for any new team).

## 3. The soundtrack loop: casuals, Britpop, grime and the concrete pitch

**Core insight:** Football style and music have run a continuous fifty-year feedback loop — terrace casuals invented European sneaker culture, Britpop resold the casual look to the world, grime made shirts and player names into lyrics, and French rap grew up inside Paris's cage-football scene — so a football shirt is never just sportswear; it is always also a music-culture garment.

**Supporting evidence:**
- The casual subculture emerged among Liverpool fans in the late 1970s, who returned from European Cup away trips with then-unknown Italian and French sportswear — Sergio Tacchini, Fila, Kappa, Lacoste, adidas trainers — partly to dodge police attention; adidas Stan Smiths, Sambas, Gazelles and Spezials became canon. (80s Casual Classics, "Casual Culture History & Terrace Subculture" — https://www.80scasualclassics.co.uk/blogs/blog/casual-culture-history-and-terrace-subculture; Highsnobiety, "How Football Casuals Became Europe's First Sneakerheads" — https://www.highsnobiety.com/p/terrace-footwear-sneaker-history/)
- Britpop revived and mainstreamed the look in the 1990s — Oasis, Blur and Pulp mixing mod revival with terrace staples (parkas, bucket hats, Gazelles, wide-leg Levi's) — decoupling casual style from hooliganism and turning it into pure aesthetics. (FashionBeans, "Football Casuals: How To Wear Terrace Fashion" — https://www.fashionbeans.com/article/terrace-fashion-guide/; ASOS fashion feed, "Football casuals: terrace style legacy" — https://www.asos.com/men/fashion-feed/2016_09_1-thurs/football-casuals-terrace-style-legacy)
- Grime and football are structurally intertwined: adidas had Stormzy announce Paul Pogba's Manchester United transfer; Dave's "Thiago Silva" Glastonbury moment (a fan pulled onstage, later acknowledged by Silva himself) went globally viral; football similes saturate grime lyrics from Skepta to AJ Tracey. (MLSSoccer.com — https://www.mlssoccer.com/post/2017/10/16/skepta-stormzy-and-best-links-between-grime-music-and-mls; Mundial, "The 11 Best Football Moments in Grime History" — https://mundialmag.com/blogs/articles/grime-fc-casisdead-kano-wiley-big-narstie; The Boar — https://theboar.org/2023/01/football-references-in-uk-grime-music/)
- The French parallel: *Ballon sur Bitume* (2016, dir. for YARD, 51 min) documented Paris-suburb cage football as a complete cultural ecosystem — technique, rap (MHD, Niska, Gradur), fashion and brands — featuring pros like Riyad Mahrez and Ousmane Dembélé who came up on those courts; for banlieue kids, "street football means freedom." (nss sports, "The importance of Ballon Sur Bitume" — https://www.nss-sports.com/en/lifestyle/9460/ballon-sur-bitume; Urban Pitch review — https://urbanpitch.com/film-review-ballon-sur-bitume-glimpse-culture-behind-french-street-football/; Fast Company on the Nike-backed production — https://www.fastcompany.com/40469632/nike-and-ad-agency-produce-new-documentary-on-paris-street-football-scene)
- The loop is still running: in 2025 Nike, PSG and Snipes staged "La Cage" in Paris — a street-football night whose billing explicitly centred "the people who design the kits, shoot the photos, paint the murals, run the pop-ups, and spin the vinyl" — reviving Total 90 / joga bonito-era street codes for a new generation. (SoccerBible — https://www.soccerbible.com/news/2025/08/shutdown-in-paris-nike-psg-snipes-take-over-for-one-night-with-la-cage/; SoccerBible, "From The Cage To The Culture" — https://www.soccerbible.com/features/2025/07/from-the-cage-to-the-culture-how-toma-el-juego-rekindles-nike-s-street-football-legacy/)

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** The cage, the five-a-side court and the Sunday pitch are where football's style innovations actually start — casuals were away-day fans, grime MCs were postcode players, Mahrez was a Sarcelles cage kid. Grassroots football is not downstream of elite football culture; historically it is upstream. Kit culture at the base of the pyramid is where the next aesthetic will come from.

**Article angles:**
1. "Europe's first sneakerheads stood on terraces" — the casuals story told as design history rather than hooligan history.
2. "From Thiago Silva to the five-a-side cage" — how UK rap turned player names into a shared vocabulary, and what that says about football as a lingua franca.
3. "Ballon sur Bitume, ten years on" — what the Paris cage scene predicted about football fashion, and who profited from it.
4. "The style loop" — a mapped essay tracing one garment (the adidas Gazelle or the oversized shirt) through terrace → Britpop stage → TikTok.

**Commercial bridge:** Football Reality (primary — the culture of the courts and terraces where MOMUTO's players actually play), Design Culture (the style-history essays).

## 4. Blokecore and the vintage shirt economy: nostalgia becomes an asset class

**Core insight:** The vintage football shirt has completed a journey from car-boot junk to global fashion object and quasi-asset class — with Classic Football Shirts' audited growth as hard proof — but the blokecore trend driving it carries an unresolved class-appropriation debate that is itself the most interesting part of the story.

**Supporting evidence:**
- Classic Football Shirts (founded Manchester, 2006, self-funded and profitable since inception): turnover up 25.6% to £24.47m in the year to June 2023, pre-tax profit more than doubling to £5.47m; revenue expected "well north of" $50m in 2024; US revenue up more than 100% year-on-year. (Footy Headlines, March 2025 — https://www.footyheadlines.com/2025/03/classic-football-shirts-made-profit-of.html; Glossy — https://www.glossy.co/fashion/classic-football-shirts-brings-its-millions-of-rare-soccer-jerseys-to-the-us/)
- In May 2024 the company took a $38.5m growth investment from The Chernin Group to expand into North America, with strategic investors including Rob McElhenney (Wrexham co-owner), Alex Morgan and Wasserman — celebrity capital validating shirts as a collectibles category. (PR Newswire — https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/classic-football-shirts-targets-immediate-expansion-into-north-america-with-significant-growth-equity-investment-from-the-chernin-group-302147297.html; Sportico — https://www.sportico.com/business/commerce/2024/alex-morgan-mcelhenney-wasserman-classic-football-shirts-1234796982/)
- "Blokecore" was coined around 2021 by TikTok creator Brandon Huntley and went viral in 2022: vintage shirt + straight-leg jeans + Sambas/Gazelles, drawing on late-20th-century British fan dress; adopted by Jennie (Blackpink), Dua Lipa, Bella Hadid and Drake, reaching the 3.Paradis SS24 runway and Balenciaga football capsules. (HBX/Hypebeast journal, "Blokecore: Where Fashion Fuses With Football," 2024 — https://hbx.com/journal/2024/05/blokecore-where-fashion-fuses-with-football; The RealReal, "Beyond Blokecore" — https://realstyle.therealreal.com/beyond-blokecore/)
- The critique: commentators argue blokecore "gentrifies" British working-class symbols — fashion elites adopting codes "without sharing their history" — while Refinery29 documented the counter-history that Black and Latina women "stylized fútbol, baseball, basketball, and hockey jerseys" decades before TikTok credited the look to white creators. (Refinery29, "Is TikTok's Blokecore Trend Cultural Appropriation?", 2023 — https://www.refinery29.com/en-us/2023/10/11560202/blokecore-fashion-trend-cultural-appropriation; Proper Mag, "Young Men are Cosplaying as Football Fans" — https://www.propermag.com/?p=92581; nss sports, "Everything is blokecore now" — https://www.nss-sports.com/en/lifestyle/34306/blokecore-football-fashion-meaning)
- The Face traced how the trade professionalised — Drake wearing Juventus, dealers, gradings and price inflation on grails. (The Face, "How vintage football shirts became big business" — https://theface.com/style/vintage-football-shirts-big-business-drake-juventus-arsenal)

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** The vintage boom demonstrates that shirts hold value through story, era and design distinctiveness — not through the level of football they were worn at. A distinctive grassroots shirt made today is a potential classic; a bland one is landfill. It also warns grassroots culture that its symbols can be strip-mined by fashion without credit — provenance and authorship matter.

**Article angles:**
1. "The £5.5m nostalgia machine" — Classic Football Shirts as a business story about what fans actually value in kit design (using CFS's growth as a dataset on which eras/designs appreciate).
2. "Who owns the bloke?" — a fair-minded essay on the blokecore appropriation debate, including the Refinery29 counter-history of Black and Latina jersey styling.
3. "Design for the archive" — what makes a shirt collectable in 20 years, reverse-engineered from the vintage market.
4. "The shirt as savings account" — collectors, grading and the moment football shirts became an asset class.

**Commercial bridge:** Design Culture (primary — this is the market context for "we document how football design evolves"), Studio Notes (what archive-worthy design means for MOMUTO's own process).

## 5. Leaks, drops and queues: the modern ritual system around a new shirt

**Core insight:** The kit release has become football's most elaborate secular ritual — a months-long cycle of leaks, verdicts, drops and queues borrowed from sneaker culture — and the ritual's centre of gravity has shifted from the club shop to the leak site, arriving months before the club's own reveal.

**Supporting evidence:**
- Footy Headlines has become the de facto global clearing house for kit leaks, publishing renders and insider images "ahead of the game," so fans routinely see kits months before official launch. (Footy Headlines — https://www.footyheadlines.com/)
- Urban Pitch argues leak culture has hollowed out release-day anticipation: where fans once first saw a kit at the club's reveal or in magazines, the surprise is now spent long before launch. (Urban Pitch, "Why Leaked Images are Spoiling the Kit Industry" — https://urbanpitch.com/kit-leaks-spoiling-industry/)
- Leaks generate their own verdict rituals: Arsenal's leaked 2026/27 home kit drew instant fan judgment ("Adidas should be more creative"); Inter's leaked 2021/22 shirt was panned on leak and praised at reveal — evidence that leak-stage opinion is a distinct, volatile phase of reception. (3 Added Minutes — https://www.3addedminutes.com/sport/football/arsenal/new-arsenal-home-kit-leaked-fan-reaction-5460607)
- Kit marketing has fully adopted sneaker drop mechanics: limited editions, fourth kits, retro re-issues, fashion-styled launches (adidas staging a World Cup kit launch in Los Angeles with fashion figures; Nike positioning 2026 World Cup kits as lifestyle items; Palace, Stüssy and Ahluwalia collaborations) — "treating the shirt as a design object rather than a uniform." (FashionNetwork, "Football themed launches come thick and fast" — https://uk.fashionnetwork.com/news/Football-themed-launches-come-thick-and-fast,1838328.html; Queue-it, "Drop Culture" — https://queue-it.com/blog/drop-culture/; Highsnobiety, "How the Football Shirt Economy Well & Truly Lost the Plot" — https://www.highsnobiety.com/p/football-shirt-kit-releases/)
- The ritual now includes revolt: by 2025, ten Premier League clubs charged £80+ for a replica shirt (Spurs £90), with consumer advocates calling it "blatant greed" and warning of a counterfeit surge; children's kit rises of up to 25% drew particular anger. (Perspective Media, "Football fans face paying up to £90 for replica shirts" — https://www.perspectivemedia.com/football-fans-face-paying-up-to-90-for-replica-shirts/)

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** The elite kit cycle is drifting away from ordinary fans — priced like luxury goods, revealed like sneaker drops, spoiled by leaks. That drift creates the opening grassroots and custom kit culture fills: shirts that are affordable, genuinely local, and whose "release day" is a real communal event (the team WhatsApp, the first training session in new kit) rather than a content calendar.

**Article angles:**
1. "The leak is the launch" — an anatomy of a modern kit release, from first Footy Headlines render to club-shop queue, and where the emotion actually happens.
2. "£90 and rising" — the replica price revolt as a story about who football shirts are for.
3. "Christmas morning, twice removed" — what fans lose when they've seen the kit six months early, and clubs' arms race against their own leaks.
4. "Drop culture comes for the badge" — how sneaker mechanics rewired kit marketing, and the point at which scarcity theatre breaks faith with supporters.

**Commercial bridge:** Football Reality (primary — the honest state of kit economics for real fans), Ready to Play Thinking (what a kit "release" should feel like for a grassroots team).

## 6. The small-club paradox: Venezia, Clapton and Dulwich out-design the giants

**Core insight:** The most culturally influential kits of the last decade have come from tiny or lower-league clubs — proof that in kit design, a coherent story beats a marketing budget, and that a shirt can carry a club's values further than its results ever will.

**Supporting evidence:**
- Venezia FC (then Serie B/A yo-yo club) became "the world's most fashion-forward football club" via 2021/22 Kappa kits designed with NYC studio Fly Nowhere under chief brand officer Ted Philipakos: the home kit sold out on day one, and since that drop ~95% of online sales have come from outside Italy — a jersey functioning, in Domus's words, as "Venice's new visual identity in the world." (VERSUS — https://www.versus.uk.com/articles/how-venezia-fc-became-the-worlds-most-fashion-forward-football-club/; Domus, 2022 — https://www.domusweb.it/en/news/2022/11/16/la-maglia-del-venezia--diventata-la-nuova-cartolina-della-citt0.html; Highsnobiety — https://www.highsnobiety.com/p/venezia-kappa-home-shirt/)
- Clapton CFC, a fan-owned non-league club in east London, released a 2018/19 away shirt in the red-yellow-purple of the Second Spanish Republic with "No Pasarán" and the International Brigades' three-pointed star; after Spanish media (including Marca) picked it up, a club expecting to sell ~250 shirts took 5,000+ pre-orders, crashed its website, and ultimately sold over 11,500 replicas. (BuzzFeed News — https://www.buzzfeed.com/patricksmith/clapton-cfc-spain-away-shirt; Clapton CFC statement, Aug 2018 — https://www.claptoncfc.co.uk/2018/08/30/were-going-to-have-to-temporarily-pause-our-shirt-pre-orders-heres-why/; Euronews — https://www.euronews.com/2018/09/18/london-football-team-sells-out-of-spanish-civil-war-shirts)
- Dulwich Hamlet (National League South) turned pink-and-blue into one of non-league's most recognisable identities, with shirts sponsored by house label Defected Records, designed by a fan (Paul Hamilton, 2024/25, inspired by the terrace song "The Hills"), made from ~16 recycled bottles each, and sold internationally — one release was among the fastest-selling non-league shirts of its season. (Goal, "The best non-league kits" — https://www.goal.com/en-gb/lists/best-non-league-kits/blt9da5962a5570d9bd; Defected Records store — https://store.defected.com/products/dulwich-hamlet-adult-2025-26-home-football-shirt)
- The counter-case proves the rule: when Venezia's design leadership moved on, SoccerBible asked "Is Venezia's Golden Era of Kit Design Over?" — noting that what had made the kits work was that "every release felt intentional, confident, and culturally fluent," not the budget. (SoccerBible, Feb 2026 — https://www.soccerbible.com/features/2026/02/venezias-new-kit-is-fine/)

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** This is the single most direct proof point in this dossier: shirt reach is uncoupled from league position. A seventh-tier club sold 11,500 shirts on the strength of a story; a Serie B club's shirt became its city's postcard. Any grassroots club with a real identity — political, musical, geographic — can make a shirt that travels. The mechanism is always the same: values → design → story → distribution.

**Article angles:**
1. "11,500 shirts for a seventh-tier club" — the Clapton CFC No Pasarán story as a case study in meaning-driven design (with the operational chaos of going viral as a fan-owned club).
2. "The postcard you can wear" — Venezia and the anatomy of a small club acting like a design house: who did what (Philipakos, Fly Nowhere, Kappa) and what actually changed.
3. "Sponsored by a record label, designed by a fan" — Dulwich Hamlet's model as the fullest expression of community kit culture.
4. "What money can't design" — why elite clubs with hundred-person brand teams keep losing the culture war to clubs with none.

**Commercial bridge:** Ready to Play Thinking (primary — the playbook for teams designing kits with meaning), Studio Notes (deconstructing how these designs were actually made), Football Reality (fan-owned club economics).
