# Grassroots Club Operations: How Amateur Football Actually Runs

**Editorial research dossier — Area 02 | MOMUTO | Researched August 2026**

**Abstract — what this area teaches us.** Amateur football is not a smaller version of professional football; it is a different organism entirely: a volunteer-run micro-institution where the same three or four people simultaneously act as CEO, CFO, compliance officer, logistics manager and procurement department — for zero pay, in the evenings, from a kitchen table. The numbers are enormous (1.4 million volunteers in England alone, 1.5 million in Germany, 8.6 million registered amateur players across 25 European countries) but the load is carried by a tiny "stalwart" core whose scarcest resource is not money or ideas but discretionary time. Academic research consistently finds that volunteer workload — not passion, not funding — is the variable that predicts satisfaction and dropout. Every operational task a club faces (affiliation, registration, safeguarding, pitch booking, sponsor management, kit procurement, payment collection) competes for the same finite pool of volunteer evenings, and almost all of those tasks land in the same compressed eight-to-ten-week summer window. This is the empirical foundation of MOMUTO's thesis: grassroots clubs don't fail for lack of ideas; they run out of decision-making time.

## TOPIC 1 — The volunteer engine: football's largest workforce is unpaid, uncounted, and shrinking

**Core insight.** Grassroots football is powered by one of the largest unpaid workforces in Europe — and that workforce peaked years ago and is now measurably contracting, which means every remaining volunteer is absorbing more operational load.

**Supporting evidence.**

- The FA reports roughly **1.4 million volunteers** keep grassroots football running in England, contributing hours valued at over £1bn per year; the FA Volunteering Workforce Survey (2018, n=1,667: 1,037 general volunteers and 630 key club and league officials) put the salary-equivalent value of adult volunteers' hours at **£1.10bn**. Sources: FA analysis of the social and economic value of adult grassroots football (https://www.thefa.com/-/media/cfa/essexfa/files/news/2021/the-social-and-economic-value-of-adult-grassroots-football-in-england.ashx); FA written evidence to Parliament (https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/111350/pdf/).
- Germany's DFB ran a national campaign thanking the **"more than 1.5 million volunteers"** behind its grassroots game across roughly **24,500 amateur clubs**; a DFB social-return study valued volunteer-generated benefits and savings at **€2.18bn** (UEFA.com, https://www.uefa.com/news-media/news/0264-10fe1c3844c9-93cbe2aefe4d-1000--dfb-campaign-thanks-1-5-million-grassroots-volunteers/).
- The foundational *Sports Volunteering in England 2002* study (Sport England, published July 2003) counted **5.82 million sport volunteers** (14% of the adult population) contributing **~1 billion hours a year — the equivalent of 720,000 full-time paid workers**, valued at over £14bn; sport was the single biggest sector of all volunteering in England (26% of volunteers). Summary report: https://www.sportengland.org/media/3383/valuing-volunteering-in-sport-in-england-summary-report.pdf; full report: https://sportengland-production-files.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/s3fs-public/valuing-volunteering-in-sport-in-england-final-report.pdf.
- The trend line points down: Sport England's Active Lives data shows **~800,000 fewer sport volunteers than before the COVID-19 pandemic and a decline of 2.1 million since 2016**, with Sport England itself noting "fewer volunteers can mean more pressure on existing volunteers" (Sport England, "Taking the long view on volunteering", https://www.sportengland.org/blogs/taking-long-view-volunteering; see also https://www.sportengland.org/taking-honest-look-volunteer-experience).
- In Germany's longitudinal Sport Development Report (Sportentwicklungsbericht, Breuer & Feiler, ninth wave), **recruiting and retaining volunteers is the single biggest problem German clubs report, and constitutes a threat to the very existence of 17.5% of clubs** — up from roughly 9% in the 2009/10 wave (DOSB, https://cdn.dosb.de/Relaunch_2024/Ehrenamt/Downloads/SEB-Bundesbericht-Englisch-bf.pdf; summary: https://cdn.dosb.de/user_upload/Sportentwicklung/Dokumente/SEB/SEB_Bericht_A5_Summary_Englisch_bf.pdf).
- Scale of the game itself: UEFA's GROW social-return-on-investment model counts **8.6 million registered amateur players across 25 European countries generating a cumulative €39.4bn annually** in economic, social and health value; the model was built by academics consulting more than 100 peer-reviewed papers (Substance, 2020, https://www.substance.net/2020/12/11/grassroots-football-participation-worth-almost-e40bn-25nations/; UEFA GROW SROI report via KNVB, https://www.knvb.nl/downloads/bestand/26276/uefa-sroi-final-report; UEFA overview, https://www.uefa.com/news-media/news/0250-0f8e6a605726-2468bc103ecc-1000--the-importance-of-social-return-on-investment/).

**Why this matters for grassroots football.** The economics of amateur football are inverted: the "product" (matches, leagues, kids playing on Sunday) is subsidised almost entirely by donated time, and that donated time is the system's scarcest, most rapidly depleting input. When the volunteer base shrinks while the compliance and admin load grows, the cost isn't visible on any balance sheet — it shows up as later kit orders, missed registration windows, and clubs quietly folding. Any product or process that consumes volunteer hours is, in effect, taxing the game's core fuel.

**Article angles.**
1. "The billion-hour game": a data-led essay on the invisible workforce that actually runs football — with the provocation that Europe's biggest football employer pays nobody.
2. "Football's slow leak": what 2.1 million lost volunteers since 2016 means for the Sunday game — reported through the lens of one club's committee.
3. "What is a volunteer hour worth?": comparing the FA's £1.1bn valuation, the DFB's €2.18bn, and UEFA's €39.4bn SROI — and what these models deliberately leave out (the 11pm WhatsApp messages).
4. "17.5%": the single German statistic that says the quiet part out loud — for one club in six, finding volunteers is now existential.

**Commercial bridge — Football Reality.** This is the macro backdrop for MOMUTO's entire thesis. If volunteer time is the game's scarcest resource, then every hour a supplier's process burns (chasing quotes, redoing designs, re-collecting sizes) is drawn from a shrinking national reserve. Football Reality content earns credibility precisely by documenting this economy of donated time with real numbers rather than sentiment — the connection to a time-saving kit process is implied by the facts, never stated as a pitch.

## TOPIC 2 — The stalwart problem: a tiny core carries the whole club, and workload is what breaks them

**Core insight.** Academic research across Europe converges on one finding: clubs survive on a small core of "stalwart" volunteers, and it is their *workload* — more than pay, recognition or ideology — that determines whether they stay or burn out.

**Supporting evidence.**

- The largest multilevel study of sports-club volunteering — **8,131 volunteers in 642 clubs across 10 European countries** — found the most important determinants of volunteer satisfaction were "the conditions of volunteering (recognition, support, leadership and material incentives) and **the workload of volunteers**" (Nagel, Seippel, Breuer, Feiler, Elmose-Østerlund, Llopis-Goig, Nichols, Perényi, Piątkowska & Scheerder, *Volunteer satisfaction in sports clubs: A multilevel analysis in 10 European countries*, International Review for the Sociology of Sport, 2020, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1012690219880419; open PDF: https://scispace.com/pdf/volunteer-satisfaction-in-sports-clubs-a-multilevel-analysis-1b104zni2y.pdf).
- Geoff Nichols (Sheffield University Management School), the UK's leading scholar of sports-club volunteering, documents the "stalwarts" phenomenon: "the reliance on a core of volunteers who do most of the work makes it harder to replace them… they are essential to maintain the club structure which allows others to take lesser volunteering roles"; a large majority of clubs report there are not enough other people willing to volunteer (Findlay-King, Nichols & Reid, written evidence to Parliament, https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/21999/pdf/; Nichols, *Volunteers in Sport: International Perspectives*, Routledge, https://www.routledge.com/Volunteers-in-Sport-International-perspectives/Nichols/p/book/9781138377905).
- The SIVSCE project — the largest comparative study of European sports clubs, **35,790 clubs across 10 countries (2015–2017)** — found that **57% of clubs believe sports clubs should be run exclusively by volunteers** (84% in Switzerland, 75% in Germany), meaning the sector ideologically resists professionalisation even as workload rises (Elmose-Østerlund et al., *Social Inclusion and Volunteering in Sports Clubs in Europe*, https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/130182/1/SIVSCE-report_final-1%20Geoff%20Nichols.pdf; project page: https://knowledge4policy.ec.europa.eu/projects-activities/sivsce-social-inclusion-volunteering-sports-clubs-europe_en; comparative report: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/317381517_Characteristics_of_European_sports_clubs_A_comparison_of_the_structure_management_voluntary_work_and_social_integration_among_sports_clubs_across_ten_European_countries).
- Organizational-capacity research shows voluntary engagement responds to club-level structures, not just individual goodwill (Wicker & Breuer et al., *The impact of organizational capacity on voluntary engagement in sports clubs: A multi-level analysis*, Sport Management Review, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1441352317300852; overview of volunteerism and volunteer management in sport: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1441352317300013).
- Practitioner-facing research echoes the pattern in blunter terms: "volunteer coaches will do 2, 3, 4 years and then burn out and drop out" (Paul Clarke, volunteer-management research interview, VolunteerPro, https://volpro.net/203-preventing-volunteer-burnout-new-research-with-paul-clarke/). Countries as different as Germany, Australia, Canada, the UK, Finland and Switzerland all report growing difficulty recruiting and retaining club volunteers (Nagel et al. 2020, above).

**Why this matters for grassroots football.** The stalwart is a single point of failure. When one secretary holds the league contacts, the FA portal login, the kit supplier relationship and the sponsor's phone number, every additional task doesn't distribute across the club — it stacks onto the same person. The research reframes burnout from a personal failing ("she just did too much") to a structural property of how clubs allocate work. It also explains why "just recruit more volunteers" fails: the core role is now so heavy and so specialised that nobody wants to inherit it.

**Article angles.**
1. "The stalwart": a profile-format piece on the person every club has — built on Nichols' research, told through one real secretary's season.
2. "Burnout is a workload problem, not a passion problem": what an 8,000-volunteer study across 10 countries actually found — against the folk theory that volunteers quit because they 'lost the love'.
3. "The succession crisis nobody plans for": what happens to a club when the one person who knows everything steps down.
4. "The 57% paradox": European clubs insist on being run by volunteers alone — while volunteer recruitment becomes their biggest existential threat. Can both beliefs survive?

**Commercial bridge — Football Reality (primary) / Ready to Play Thinking (secondary).** The stalwart's constraint is cognitive load and hours, which makes the empirical case that reducing decisions — not adding options — is what helps clubs. Ready to Play Thinking can honestly build on this: a wall of finished, customizable designs is a workload intervention for the exact person this research describes, because it converts an open-ended design project (meetings, opinions, revisions) into a single decision. The connection is inevitable because the research names workload, not aesthetics, as the breaking point.

## TOPIC 3 — Kitchen-table governance: clubs are institutions run like households, and the committee is the bottleneck

**Core insight.** Most amateur clubs are governed informally — "kitchen-table" style — by a legally mandated skeleton of three or four officers, so every club decision, from safeguarding to shirt colours, must pass through the same overloaded micro-committee.

**Supporting evidence.**

- The FA's affiliation process requires every club to appoint named **"Key Officials" — Chairperson, Secretary, Treasurer, and Club Welfare Officer** — and only these individuals can complete affiliation; a Club Welfare Officer is mandatory for clubs with youth sections, adult-team clubs need a named Welfare Officer (Adult Teams), and open-age team officials must complete the Player Welfare Course as part of affiliation (FA Grassroots Technology, "Affiliation Requirements", https://grassrootstechnology.thefa.com/support/solutions/articles/48001235370-affiliation-requirements; Amateur FA club roles, https://www.amateur-fa.com/clubs-and-competitions/players-and-people/roles; FA guide to creating a club, https://www.thefa.com/-/media/cfa/amateurfa/files/setting-up-a-club-v1.ashx).
- Research applying Bourdieu to community sports clubs in New Zealand found clubs deliberately retained "a traditional 'kitchen-table' approach with no or little evidence of formalization and professionalization", with committees reproducing "a habitus that prized social and symbolic capital over economic and cultural capital" — i.e., informality is not backwardness but a cultural choice (*Around the kitchen-table with Bourdieu*, Sport in Society, 2019/2021, https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17430437.2019.1615893).
- The UK Sports Governance Academy publishes a dedicated framework (the "7Fs") for grassroots club governance precisely because standard corporate governance doesn't map onto volunteer committees (Sports Governance Academy, *Organisational governance & grassroots sports clubs: The 7Fs Framework*, https://www.sportsgovernanceacademy.org.uk/media/mlcp4gbu/sga-organisational-governance-grassroots-def-final-full-0411.pdf).
- The secretary role is where the load concentrates: FA-aligned guidance describes it as "arguably the largest list of roles and responsibilities" — AGM agendas, minutes, all FA and league correspondence, and the club database of teams, managers, grounds and leagues (Junior Grassroots Hub, https://www.juniorgrassrootshub.com/news/grassroots-football-club-roles); club-software vendor TeamStats claims "the average grassroots club secretary spends 12–15 hours per week on admin tasks that could take two" (vendor figure, treat as indicative: TeamStats, https://www.teamstats.net/blog/building-a-modern-grassroots-club-structure).
- Nichols and James found "one size does not fit all": voluntary clubs vary so much in structure that policy (and, by extension, any supplier) that assumes a standardised, professional organisation on the other end will misfire (Nichols & James, 2008, discussed in *Great Expectations: Voluntary Sports Clubs and Their Role in Delivering National Policy for English Sport*, Voluntas, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/voluntas/article/abs/great-expectations-voluntary-sports-clubs-and-their-role-in-delivering-national-policy-for-english-sport/537437464CE04A7EEF45B51688EBD191).

**Why this matters for grassroots football.** The committee is where time goes to die — not because volunteers are inefficient, but because consensus among part-time unpaid officers is inherently slow, and the FA's structure funnels legal responsibility through a handful of named individuals. A kit decision at a grassroots club is not a purchase; it's a governance event: it touches the treasurer (budget), the secretary (supplier contact), the chair (sponsor relationship) and often a parents' WhatsApp group (opinions). Understanding this explains why clubs take weeks to make decisions a company would make in an hour — and why the delay is structural, not personal.

**Article angles.**
1. "The kitchen table": inside the governance model that runs European football's base layer — an anthropological piece drawing on the Bourdieu study, told without condescension.
2. "Four names on a form": how the FA's Key Officials requirement quietly defines who really runs English football.
3. "Why your club takes six weeks to decide anything": committee mathematics — the honest mechanics of consensus among volunteers who meet once a month.
4. "The secretary is the club": an essay on the role with the longest job description in football and no salary.

**Commercial bridge — Ready to Play Thinking.** This topic is the direct evidence base for MOMUTO's claim that decision-making, not production, is the bottleneck. If a club's decision cycle is one committee meeting per month, then a kit process requiring three rounds of approvals costs a quarter of a season before production even starts. Ready to Play — finished designs that need one yes instead of many — is an answer shaped exactly like the problem the governance literature describes. The bridge is inevitable because the bottleneck is documented, not asserted.

## TOPIC 4 — The compressed calendar: almost everything a club must do happens in the same ten summer weeks

**Core insight.** Affiliation, league entry, player registration, safeguarding renewals, pitch booking and kit procurement all fall between early June and early September — a period when the FA's own systems go offline for the season switch and when kit suppliers are at peak capacity — so a club's whole year is decided in a window shorter than a school holiday.

**Supporting evidence.**

- FA systems open **affiliation and player registration on 1 June** for the upcoming season; the Whole Game System and related platforms take scheduled **downtime around 1–5 July** to switch seasons; player registration through the FA Club Portal has been **mandatory for all affiliated leagues since 2021/22**, and every player must be registered to club, team and league with a unique email (FA Grassroots Technology, "Key Dates", https://grassrootstechnology.thefa.com/support/solutions/articles/48001235371-key-dates-2024; Affiliation FAQs, https://grassrootstechnology.thefa.com/support/solutions/articles/48001238121-affiliation-faqs; FA registrations information, https://www.thefa.com/football-rules-governance/policies/player-registration/registrations-information; Kent FA player registration, https://www.kentfa.com/leagues-and-clubs/player-registration).
- Affiliation is itself conditional: for 2026/27 it requires an accepted Safeguarding Declaration, and named officials of open-age teams must complete the Player Welfare Course — training that must be chased from volunteers during their summer holidays (FA Grassroots Technology affiliation requirements, https://grassrootstechnology.thefa.com/support/solutions/articles/48001235370-affiliation-requirements; Berks & Bucks FA affiliation, https://www.berks-bucksfa.com/leagues-and-clubs/club-management/affiliation).
- Standard pre-season checklists pile league paperwork, entry fees, pitch booking, first-aid cover, safeguarding renewals, kit audit and volunteer recruitment into the same July–August period — while noting that "pre-season coincides with school holidays, so players aren't always available, and large numbers of absences in July and August make planning difficult" (TeamStats, "Grassroots Football Checklist: New Season Prep", https://www.teamstats.net/blog/grassroots-football-getting-ready-new-season-checklist).
- Kit supply hits its capacity crunch at exactly this moment. Industry guidance: **4–8 weeks production** is typical for custom kit; "six weeks is the floor, leaving no room for a hiccup"; suppliers advise ordering **three to four months early** and warn that during the **June–September peak "suppliers can be slammed with orders in the same few-week window"** and oversell capacity (Podiumwear, "Will My Custom Soccer Jerseys Arrive On Time?", https://www.podiumwear.com/2026/07/will-my-custom-soccer-jerseys-arrive-on-time/; Luson Sport, "Custom Soccer Jersey Lead Time", https://luson.com/custom-soccer-jersey-lead-time/; Pillar Sports ordering guide, https://pillarsports.com/how-to-order-custom-sports-uniforms-club-school-team/; O'Neills published lead times, https://teamwear.oneills.com/en/leadtime).
- The sequencing trap: a club cannot finalise kit numbers until the squad is settled, but squads settle late because registration opens 1 June and players drift back in August — precisely when supplier lead times have stretched. Suppliers themselves note "you're almost guaranteed to have a late player, a lost jersey, or an add-on mid-season" (Pillar Sports, https://pillarsports.com/how-to-order-custom-sports-uniforms-club-school-team/; Print Reloaded, https://printreloaded.com/blogs/workwear/custom-football-team-kits-what-you-need-to-know-before-order).

**Why this matters for grassroots football.** The grassroots year has a structural design flaw: the decision window and the delivery window overlap. A club that starts its kit conversation in July — a perfectly reasonable moment, since that's when squads and budgets firm up — is already inside most suppliers' minimum lead time for a September kickoff. The result every August: teams starting seasons in last year's shirts, mismatched shorts, or borrowed kit. This is rarely anyone's negligence; it is calendar arithmetic. Documenting that arithmetic honestly (dates, lead times, downtime windows) is more useful to clubs than another "order early!" scold.

**Article angles.**
1. "The ten weeks that decide your season": an annotated timeline of the grassroots summer — 1 June to kickoff — mapping every FA deadline, system downtime, safeguarding course and supplier lead time onto one calendar.
2. "Why every club orders kit late (and why it's not their fault)": the sequencing trap between squad registration and supplier lead times, explained with dates.
3. "The week football's software goes dark": the odd annual ritual of Whole Game System downtime, and what it reveals about how the amateur game is administered.
4. "August is the cruelest month": a reported diary of one club's pre-season, task by task.

**Commercial bridge — Ready to Play Thinking (primary) / Football Reality (secondary).** MOMUTO's operational facts (production 7–12 days, door-to-door 25–30 days, designs already finished) only mean something against this calendar. Editorially, the story is the calendar itself: when the industry's floor is six weeks plus a design cycle, the only two levers a club controls are (a) how early it decides and (b) how few decisions the process needs. Ready to Play Thinking is the second lever articulated as a philosophy; the compressed-calendar article practically writes the case study.

## TOPIC 5 — Sizes, logos and subs: the unglamorous supply chain inside every club

**Core insight.** The three humble data-collection tasks behind every kit order — player sizes, sponsor artwork, and money — are each a distributed chase across dozens of households, and together they, not the manufacturer, usually set the real lead time.

**Supporting evidence.**

- **Sizes.** Suppliers require a complete size list before confirming an order; industry guidance is to "build a simple squad measurement spreadsheet before contacting any supplier — most manufacturers require this before confirming a quote, and having it ready cuts your lead time by a week or more", and to order sample sizes first because youth players "may have grown since the previous season" (Pillar Sports, https://pillarsports.com/how-to-order-custom-sports-uniforms-club-school-team/; TeamStats new-season checklist, https://www.teamstats.net/blog/grassroots-football-getting-ready-new-season-checklist; Print Reloaded, https://printreloaded.com/blogs/workwear/custom-football-team-kits-what-you-need-to-know-before-order).
- **Sponsor artwork.** Clubs must secure permission to use sponsor logos and supply high-quality artwork files; low-resolution logos and placement disputes are a standard cause of proof-stage delays, and "mistakes caught at proof stage cost nothing to fix while mistakes after production are expensive" — which is why the digital proof must be "walked past club leadership or kit sponsors before signing off", adding another approval loop through the committee (Print Reloaded, https://printreloaded.com/blogs/workwear/custom-football-team-kits-what-you-need-to-know-before-order; The Soccer Factory, https://thesoccerfactory.com/blogs/news/what-to-look-for-when-ordering-custom-soccer-uniforms; Podiumwear, https://www.podiumwear.com/2026/07/will-my-custom-soccer-jerseys-arrive-on-time/). Last-minute sponsor swaps close to kickoff are common enough that suppliers describe the roster-release-to-season-opening window as "a critical high-risk phase" (WalterFootball/GSJJ white paper, https://walterfootball.com/footballmerchandisewhitepaper.php).
- **Money.** Payment-provider research (vendor data, but directionally consistent): "80% of organisations wait up to 20 days past the due date for manual payments"; "the typical football club treasurer or admin volunteer often spends over a quarter of their time on billing processes"; one club recovered "£300–400 a month in previously missed revenue" after automating collection; GoCardless alone works with 18 County FAs and 1,500+ clubs — evidence of how widespread the chase is (GoCardless, https://gocardless.com/blog/four-ways-manual-payments-are-hurting-grassroots-football-clubs; https://gocardless.com/blog/late-payments-are-a-cash-flow-killer; https://gocardless.com/blog/three-ways-grassroots-football-clubs-can-reduce-their-admin-burden; Total Grassroots/Kelme/GoCardless partnership, https://ffnews.com/newsarticle/paytech/total-grassroots-and-kelme-partner-with-gocardless-to-offer-stress-free-payments-and-new-kits-to-grassroots-football-clubs/).
- **The aggregate picture.** LoveAdmin's 2025 Big Grassroots Football Survey (volunteers across 27 UK FAs) found the defining pain points are administrative: "volunteers are busy and don't want to spend hours chasing payments or filling in forms" (LoveAdmin, https://loveadmin.com/resources/guides/grassroots-football-survey/). The FA's treasurer role description makes the chase official: the treasurer must "pay all expenses, collect monies owed and keep a record of accounts" (Amateur FA roles, https://www.amateur-fa.com/clubs-and-competitions/players-and-people/roles).

**Why this matters for grassroots football.** A kit order looks like one transaction but is actually ~30 micro-transactions: a size, a spelling of a name, a number preference, and a payment from every household, plus an artwork file and an approval from a sponsor. Each micro-transaction has a response-time measured in days, and they run in series through one volunteer's phone. This is why the real lead time of a grassroots kit order is "supplier production time plus four to six weeks of human herding" — and why the herding, being invisible, never gets planned for. The insight generalises: most grassroots "delays" are actually distributed-data-collection problems.

**Article angles.**
1. "Thirty families, one spreadsheet": the anatomy of a grassroots kit order, told as a logistics story — every message, chase and correction between 'let's get new kit' and the box arriving.
2. "The £300 a month that goes missing": how informal payment collection quietly bleeds amateur clubs — and why treasurers burn out chasing it.
3. "The JPEG that delayed the season": a small, true story format about sponsor logo files — what a 40kb screenshot of a logo does to a production schedule.
4. "Herding is the lead time": why the slowest part of any club order is never the factory.

**Commercial bridge — Studio Notes (primary) / Football Reality (secondary).** This is MOMUTO's own operational territory: sizes, artwork files, proofs and approvals are precisely what its studio handles daily, so Studio Notes can document these mechanics from the inside with real examples (what a print-ready logo actually is; how size curves behave in youth squads; what a clean order looks like). Written as craft documentation rather than promotion, it earns authority on the exact process the reader is struggling with — and quietly demonstrates why a process designed around fewer, cleaner inputs (Ready to Play) exists.

## Cross-cutting editorial note

The strongest through-line in the research: **workload is the load-bearing variable.** The academic literature (Nagel et al. 2020; Nichols; SIVSCE; Sportentwicklungsbericht) says it determines volunteer survival; the calendar analysis shows workload spikes exactly when supplier capacity is tightest; the payments/sizes/logos mechanics show where the hours actually go. An editorial programme that takes the volunteer's hour as its unit of analysis — what consumes it, what it's worth, who is spending it — will be distinctive, defensible, and structurally aligned with MOMUTO's thesis without ever needing to mention the brand.
