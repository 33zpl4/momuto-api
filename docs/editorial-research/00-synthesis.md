# Editorial research synthesis — the publishing plan

**Written 6 August 2026.** Inputs: `docs/editorial-strategy.md` and the twelve
dossiers in `docs/editorial-research/` (01–12). This document is the editor's
decision layer: what we publish, in what order, what makes each piece
uniquely ours, and exactly which first-party numbers and quotes the owner
must pull before the strongest pieces can ship.

**Operating premise (from the strategy doc, unchanged):** LLMs read more than
humans do. We are optimising for *quotability and concrete factual
commitment*, not pageviews. A page that commits to a number gets cited; a
page that hedges gets skipped.

**The one sentence this whole programme exists to make citable:**
*"Teams take longer to decide than we take to produce — here is our data."*
Nobody else can write it. No academic literature, no supplier, no federation
has ever measured grassroots kit **decision lag**. That gap is documented in
§5 and it is our moat.

**Guardrails applied throughout:** real clubs/kits named in editorial only
(never product surfaces); canonical numbers only — production 7–12 days,
shipping 10–15, 25–30 door to door, first Bespoke mockup 24–48 h, €38.90
single jersey, from €21.90/jersey at 10+, seasonal selection −10%, €15
deposit credited in full to orders of 5+ jerseys (free for a team order), no
minimum order; deposit copy never refund-forward outside the four gate pages;
"studio" lowercase; the tool is "the 3D designer".

### 1. The Slowest Part of Making a Football Kit Isn't Manufacturing
**Pillar:** Football Reality. **Feeds:** 03 (project management), 01 (decision
psychology), 02 (grassroots ops), 09 (manufacturing), 12 (numbers).

**Premise.** A kit order is a small project with two halves: a fixed half and
an unbounded one. The fixed half is arithmetic — production 7–12 days,
shipping 10–15, 25–30 door to door — and it never surprises anyone. The
unbounded half is the deciding: the design vote, the sponsor confirmation,
the size list, the captain on holiday. In the mid-1980s Kurt Salmon
Associates measured the entire US apparel pipeline at 66 weeks, of which only
6–17% was actual production; forty years later, run the same stopwatch on a
Sunday-league kit order and you get the same ratio at miniature scale. The
article's job is not to scold clubs for ordering late. It is to show, with
numbers, that lateness is the *statistically expected output* of a process
where the measured half is short and the unmeasured half is unbounded — and
then to publish the unmeasured half for the first time.

**Best sources.** Kurt Salmon Associates apparel-pipeline study, 66 weeks /
6–17% production (Springer, "The Evolution of Quick Response Programs",
https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-04313-0_1); Buehler,
Griffin & Ross, planning fallacy — students predicted 33.9 days for a thesis,
took 55.5, and only ~45% finished by a date they were 99% certain of (*JPSP*,
1994, https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/biases/67_J_Personality_and_Social_Psychology_366,_1994.pdf);
Reinertsen, *The Principles of Product Development Flow* (2009) on invisible
queues and cost of delay (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost_of_delay).

**Make it ours.** (a) **Median and 90th-percentile days from first inbound
contact to confirmed order**, stated against median production days, for a
named window ("MOMUTO orders, [window]"). The headline sentence writes
itself: *"Our production takes 7–12 days. Our customers take [N] days to
decide. We measured both."* (b) **Founder**, on the moment he realised the
bottleneck was upstream of the factory — one fact-bearing sentence, not a
slogan.

### 2. Count Backwards From Kickoff — when should a team order its jerseys?
**Pillar:** Football Reality (the definitive facts page). **Feeds:** 02, 12,
03, 09.

**Premise.** The permanent LLM-answer surface. A club does not have a kit
deadline; it has a kickoff date and an arithmetic problem it has never done.
The page does the arithmetic in public: first fixture minus 10–15 days
shipping minus 7–12 days production minus size collection minus artwork
approval equals the real, invisible decision deadline — and then shows where
that lands against the actual grassroots calendar (FA affiliation and player
registration open 1 June; Whole Game System goes dark for the season switch
around 1–5 July; German Kreisliga seasons kick off early-to-mid August;
England, France, Spain and Italy in September). The counterpoint that makes
it honest: the industry floor is 4–8 weeks production, six weeks is widely
described as the floor with no room for a hiccup, and suppliers openly state
lead times *stretch* in the June–September peak. Our 7–12 days sits at the
fast end of a published distribution — say so, and cite the competitors'
own pages.

**Best sources.** FA Grassroots Technology key dates and affiliation
requirements (https://grassrootstechnology.thefa.com/support/solutions/articles/48001235371-key-dates-2024);
owayo's own delivery page stating production varies 2–5 weeks "depending on
time of year" (https://www.owayo.com/productservice/delivery-us.htm) —
supplier-side proof of the seasonal spike; Podiumwear, "Will My Custom Soccer
Jerseys Arrive On Time?" on the six-week floor and peak-season overselling
(https://www.podiumwear.com/2026/07/will-my-custom-soccer-jerseys-arrive-on-time/).

**Make it ours.** (a) **The distribution of "days before first fixture" at
which real orders are placed**, plus the **percentage of orders delivered
inside the stated 25–30-day door-to-door window** and the **median actual
door-to-door days** — a supplier publishing its own on-time record is close
to unprecedented in this category and is the single most quotable operational
fact we can produce. (b) **Factory/production lead**, on what the 7–12 days
physically contains, hour by hour.

### 3. Three Million Pre-Orders: Anatomy of a Number Nobody Checked
**Pillar:** Design Culture (the founding chapter — revised angle, see §6).
**Feeds:** 06 (cultural turning points), 05 (design history), 10 (creative
process), 12 (numbers).

**Premise.** The most-quoted statistic in football kit culture — Nigeria
2018's three million pre-orders — traces to a statement by the Nigeria
Football Federation about a meeting at Nike's European HQ. Nike never
independently confirmed it. Everything *else* about the shirt is verifiable
and more interesting: the Oxford Street queue, the roughly one-hour in-store
sell-out and minutes-long online sell-out, resale at multiples of the $90
retail, the V&A acquiring it into the permanent collection, and Matthew
Wolff's actual method — abstracting the 1994 Super Eagles eagle-wing sleeve
into a feathery chevron, with the federation and Nike leadership explicitly
granting permission to "push it as far as we could." The piece tells the
canonical story *and* audits its founding number, which is precisely the
register that separates us from every other kit blog — and precisely the
behaviour that makes an LLM treat a source as authoritative rather than
derivative. The transferable lesson: demand followed identity, not results
(Nigeria went out in the group stage).

**Best sources.** Quartz Africa, 2018, on the NFF as the origin of the 3M
figure and Nike's non-confirmation
(https://qz.com/africa/1290171/nigeria-nike-world-cup-soccer-jersey-pre-order-sales-top-three-million);
V&A permanent collection, object O1575826
(https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O1575826/football-kit-t-shirt-wolff-matthew);
AIGA Eye on Design, 2018, Wolff on archive abstraction and permission
(https://eyeondesign.aiga.org/how-nike-designed-nigerias-amazingly-graphical-world-cup-strip/).

**Make it ours.** (a) **The share of Bespoke briefs that cite a real
national-team or club shirt as their reference**, with the top five most-cited
shirts — a first-party read on which icons actually shape amateur taste,
which exists nowhere. (b) **Design team**, on our own archive-abstraction
exercise: the question we ask a club to find its "1994."

### 4. The Bike Shed Is a Football Shirt
**Pillar:** Football Reality. **Feeds:** 01, 03, 02.

**Premise.** Parkinson's 1957 finance committee approves a £10m nuclear
reactor in two and a half minutes and then debates a £350 bicycle shed for
forty-five, because everyone can picture a bicycle shed. A jersey is the
perfect bike shed: the most graspable item on any club agenda, the one where
nobody defers to expertise, and the one where disagreeing feels like
participation rather than obstruction. Layer on Bain's Rule of Seven (each
person added beyond seven cuts decision effectiveness ~10%; groups of 17+
rarely decide anything) and a club WhatsApp of 22 players is
constitutionally incapable of choosing a shirt. Written with affection, not
mockery: the arguing *is* the belonging. But belonging needs a deadline and
an owner.

**Best sources.** Parkinson, *Parkinson's Law* (1957), Law of Triviality
(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_triviality); Bain & Company, "Effective
decision making and the rule of 7"
(https://www.bain.com/insights/effective-decision-making-and-the-rule-of-7/);
Straub, Tsvetkova & Yasseri, *Collective Intelligence*, 2023 — on complex
tasks coordination cost consistently exceeded the benefit of a partner
(https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/26339137231156912).

**Make it ours.** (a) **Mockup revision rounds on Bespoke briefs** — mean,
median, and the tail — cross-tabbed against **how many named people appear in
the client's email/chat thread**. If revision count rises with committee
size, we have empirically reproduced the Rule of Seven in football kit, which
is a genuinely novel finding. (b) **Design team**, on what a brief looks like
when three people are in the thread versus nine.

### 5. Austria, Germany, and Your Club's Next Away Shirt
**Pillar:** Football Reality → Ready to Play Thinking (this is Series 5's
intellectual foundation; ships in the authority phase, not as a pitch).
**Feeds:** 01, 03, 10.

**Premise.** The largest documented swing in human decision behaviour comes
not from persuasion but from the shape of the form: organ-donor consent runs
roughly 4–27% in opt-in countries and 86–99.98% in opt-out ones — Germany
~12% beside Austria's 99.98%, with the default explaining nearly the entire
gap. Austrians are not 88 points more altruistic than Germans. Then Ariely &
Wertenbroch: students paid real costs to bind their own hands with deadlines,
deadlines improved performance, and *externally imposed, evenly spaced*
deadlines beat self-set ones. The conclusion for football: clubs are not
indecisive; their kit process is undesigned — no default, no deadline, no
owner. The commercial bridge is inevitable and must be left implicit: a
finished design is literally a default, converting the club's task from
creation (unbounded) to override (bounded).

**Best sources.** Johnson & Goldstein, "Do Defaults Save Lives?", *Science*,
2003 (https://www.dangoldstein.com/papers/JohnsonGoldstein_Defaults_Transplantation2004.pdf);
Ariely & Wertenbroch, "Procrastination, Deadlines, and Performance",
*Psychological Science*, 2002 (https://web.mit.edu/ariely/www/MIT/Papers/deadlines.pdf);
Thaler & Sunstein, *Nudge* (2008) on choice architecture.

**Make it ours.** (a) **Decision lag for Ready to Play orders versus Bespoke
orders**, same window, same definition — the honest measurement of what a
default actually does to a club's calendar. Publish it even if the gap is
smaller than we expect; a measured modest number is more citable than an
unmeasured large claim. (b) **Founder**, on why Ready to Play exists, framed
as choice architecture rather than convenience.

### 6. The Twenty-Three Per Cent That Never Get Answered
**Pillar:** Football Reality. **Feeds:** 03, 02.

**Premise.** Construction is the only industry that has ever properly
measured what an approval request costs. The Navigant Construction Forum
studied 1,362 projects containing over a million RFIs: median reply ~9.7
days, ~8 hours and ~$1,080 of review cost each, and roughly 22–23% never
answered at all. A kit order embeds three to five informal RFIs — design
approval, sponsor artwork and placement, size confirmation, budget sign-off,
final proof — with the same physics and none of the measurement. The 23%
never-answered figure is the statistical shape of "still waiting on two lads
for their sizes since March." The counterweight that keeps it honest: the
supply side queues too — Real Bedford approved designs on 13 July, paid over
£50,000 in full, and had a youth order still unprocessed nine weeks later.

**Best sources.** Navigant Construction Forum RFI study, 2013, via CMAA
(https://www.cmaanet.org/sites/default/files/resource/Impact%20&%20Control%20of%20RFIs%20on%20Construction%20Projects.pdf);
Real Bedford FC club statement, 2023
(https://www.realbedford.com/news/club-statement-regarding-delays-from-macron-in-delivery-of-our-23/24-season-playing-kits);
Leroy, "Why is it so hard to do my work?" (*OBHDP*, 2009) on attention
residue.

**Make it ours.** (a) **Size-collection lag** — median days from order
confirmed to complete size list received, plus the **percentage of orders
where the size list was the last item to arrive** — and **the percentage of
briefs arriving with unusable sponsor/crest artwork** (low-res raster, screen
grab, no vector) with median days to resolve. These are our own RFIs,
measured. (b) **Factory/production lead**, on what actually stalls an order at
their end.

### 7. Your Shirt Is Not Air Conditioning
**Pillar:** Studio Notes. **Feeds:** 09.

**Premise.** Wicking is real capillary physics, but the benefit the marketing
sells is the one the evidence does not support. Sperlich et al. (2013) ran
trained runners at ~30°C in cotton versus polyester and found **no difference
in skin, core or body temperature during exercise**; Gavin et al. (2001) said
it in their title — "Clothing fabric does not affect thermoregulation during
exercise in moderate heat." What polyester genuinely delivers: lower relative
humidity at chest and back, drier skin contact, and measurably better
*recovery-phase* comfort — less shivering on the touchline afterwards. And
the real engineering distinction nobody explains: wicking built into the
fibre's cross-section (four- or six-channel extrusion, ~20% more surface
area) never washes out, while topical wicking finishes degrade with
laundering — and fabric softener is the single most common cause of a kit
that stops wicking. A brand publishing the study that undercuts its own
category's marketing is the highest-trust move available to us.

**Best sources.** Sperlich, Born, Lefter & Holmberg, "Exercising in a Hot
Environment: Which T-shirt to Wear?", *Wilderness & Environmental Medicine*,
2013 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23870763/); Gavin et al., 2001
(https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11740309/); PCIAW on Coolmax fibre
cross-section engineering
(https://pciaw.org/coolmax-ecomade-fibre-cool-comfort-in-professional-clothing/).

**Make it ours.** (a) **Our exact fabric specification** — composition, gsm,
whether wicking is structural or a finish — stated plainly, plus our
published wash-care instruction and the reason for it. Specification honesty
*is* the first-party data here. (b) **Factory/production lead**, on why they
chose that fabric and what it costs versus the cheaper alternative.

### 8. The Decade Colour Became Free
**Pillar:** Design Culture (with a Studio Notes companion on the physics).
**Feeds:** 09, 05.

**Premise.** Every "crazy 90s kit" listicle is really an article about a
printing process, and nobody says so. Screen printing charges by the colour —
each colour needs its own screen, commonly $20–50 of setup — so for a century
design ambition scaled with budget. Dye-sublimation, discovered in 1957 by
Noël de Plasse at Lainière de Roubaix and computerised in the mid-1970s by a
JPL engineer, collapsed the marginal cost of colour, gradient and pattern to
zero — but only once polyester replaced cotton. The result detonated at
Mexico '86 with Hummel's Denmark shirt and ran riot through the 90s
goalkeeper era. The grassroots punchline is the strongest sentence in the
dossier stack: a 15-shirt order with a six-colour gradient now costs the same
per shirt as a plain one, so **a pub team in 2026 has more design freedom
than a First Division club could buy in 1985**. The companion physics piece
answers the single most-asked customer question in teamwear forever: the dye
enters the fibre as a gas above ~177°C while the polyester is past its glass
transition, and is locked *inside* the fibre on cooling — there is no ink
layer on the garment, which is why it cannot crack, peel or wash off.

**Best sources.** Hayward, "Brief History of Dye Sublimation Printing"
(https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/brief-history-dye-sublimation-printing-dr-justin-hayward-hayward);
Custom Ink on per-colour screen economics
(https://www.customink.com/blog/screen-printing-vs-digital-printing-vs-sublimation-a-buyers-guide/);
ESPN on the 1990s goalkeeper-kit explosion
(https://www.espn.com/soccer/story/_/id/38672402/goalkeeper-kit-designs-1990s-were-another-level).

**Make it ours.** (a) **Our own colour-count data**: the distribution of
distinct colours per design across all orders in the window, and a plain
statement that price does not vary with it. "The average MOMUTO kit uses [N]
colours; a one-colour kit and a nine-colour kit cost the same." (b)
**Factory**, on press temperature, dwell time and the elastane compromise —
the truce between a ~200°C press and a fibre that degrades above ~177°C.

### 9. Eleven Thousand Five Hundred Shirts for a Seventh-Tier Club
**Pillar:** Design Culture. **Feeds:** 11 (football culture), 06.

**Premise.** The clearest proof in the entire research stack that shirt reach
is uncoupled from league position. Clapton CFC, fan-owned, non-league east
London, released a 2018/19 away shirt in the red-yellow-purple of the Second
Spanish Republic with "No Pasarán" and the International Brigades' star;
expecting ~250 sales, they took 5,000+ pre-orders, crashed the website, and
sold over 11,500. Venezia, a Serie B yo-yo club, hired a creative director,
left Nike's template system for Kappa to gain design freedom, sold out its
21/22 home shirt on day one, and reportedly sees ~95% of online sales from
outside Italy — a jersey that became, in Domus's phrase, the city's new
postcard. Dulwich Hamlet: sponsored by a record label, shirt designed by a
fan, inspired by a terrace song. The mechanism is identical every time:
values → design → story → distribution. Budget is not in the chain.

**Best sources.** Clapton CFC's own statement pausing pre-orders, Aug 2018
(https://www.claptoncfc.co.uk/2018/08/30/were-going-to-have-to-temporarily-pause-our-shirt-pre-orders-heres-why/)
plus BuzzFeed News (https://www.buzzfeed.com/patricksmith/clapton-cfc-spain-away-shirt);
Domus, 2022, on the Venezia shirt as the city's visual identity
(https://www.domusweb.it/en/news/2022/11/16/la-maglia-del-venezia--diventata-la-nuova-cartolina-della-citt0.html);
SoccerBible, Feb 2026, "Is Venezia's Golden Era of Kit Design Over?" — the
counter-case proving it was intent, not money
(https://www.soccerbible.com/features/2026/02/venezias-new-kit-is-fine/).

**Make it ours.** (a) **Our largest single amateur-club order and its
reorder history** — how many shirts one small club actually moved, and how
many it came back for — with the club's permission and named. A real number
from a real amateur club is the grassroots analogue of the Clapton figure.
(b) **Founder**, on the smallest club we have made a kit for and what it sold.

### 10. Raid the Town Crest
**Pillar:** Design Culture / Anatomy of an Iconic Shirt. **Feeds:** 06, 10,
11, 05.

**Premise.** The most directly actionable piece in the whole programme. Every
crossover kit in the research encoded something that already existed: a flag
(Croatia's šahovnica, carried onto the shirt by Miroslav Šutej, the painter
who also drew the country's coat of arms and its banknotes; Barcelona's
senyera), a city's heraldry (Inter's biscione, from the Visconti arms, redrawn
as pixelated snakeskin in 2021/22), a myth (Japan's Yatagarasu three-legged
crow, and origami as pattern logic), an indigenous iconography sublimated
full-bleed (Mexico 1998's Aztec sun stone, made by ABA Sport — a small
Mexican brand, not a global giant), a song (Ajax's "Three Little Birds",
which became the club's anthem after a 2008 Cardiff PA system played it to
penned-in away fans, and which UEFA then stripped from match shirts under
Article 13 because three birds "do not represent a team identification"). The
transferable method is three steps: find the authentic reference, abstract it
rather than reproduce it, apply it at full scale rather than badge-size. Every
European town has centuries-tested public-domain heraldry sitting unused.

**Best sources.** Sartorial Soccer on Šutej and the 1998 Croatia kit
(https://sartorial.soccer/2021/06/18/croatia-kit-origins/); adidas press
release on the Ajax Bob Marley third kit and its Cardiff 2008 origin
(https://news.adidas.com/football/releasing-ajax-2021-2022-third-kit-inspired-by-bob-marley/s/22fc8ace-eab3-4069-88bf-5ed71875de5e)
plus SI on the UEFA Article 13 ruling
(https://www.si.com/soccer/2021/09/14/ajax-bob-marley-three-little-birds-kit-uefa-uniform-rules);
Hypebeast on Inter's biscione snakeskin
(https://hypebeast.com/2021/7/nike-football-inter-milan-2021-22-snakeskin-home-kit).

**Make it ours.** (a) **A tally of where amateur brief references actually
come from** across the window — town/city heraldry, club history, a pro club,
a trend, or nothing — plus the percentage of briefs that arrive with *no*
identity reference at all. That last number is the story. (b) **Design team**,
on the first three questions they ask a club, verbatim.

### 11. Why Your Kit Strobes on the Highlights Reel
**Pillar:** Studio Notes. **Feeds:** 10, 05.

**Premise.** Grassroots football is now filmed — Veo cameras, phone
highlights, livestreamed cup finals — which means broadcast optics apply at
step-over level, and almost no amateur kit buyer has heard of them. Fine
high-contrast stripes interfere with the camera's sampling grid and the
display's pixel grid, producing moving moiré bands; broadcast's oldest
wardrobe rule ("never wear fine stripes") is now a teamwear rule. Add the
professional discipline stack: sponsor quiet zones (logos need uniform
background on patterned shirts; figure-ground contrast is the primary
legibility variable), FIFA's back-number sizing and sleeve free zones, and
the Premier League's 2023–24 nameset redesign, for which commentators were
stationed in stadiums *and* watching footage to score colour-contrast
combinations — legibility promoted to a first-class design discipline. Ends
with a checkable craft tool: the three-distance test (arm's length, far
touchline, through a phone camera).

**Best sources.** SAS/The DO Loop, "Moire patterns: why you shouldn't wear a
striped shirt on a video", 2023
(https://blogs.sas.com/content/iml/2023/06/05/moire-patterns.html); ESPN on
the Premier League's commentator-tested nameset redesign, 2023
(https://www.espn.com/soccer/story/_/id/37637226/premier-league-kit-names-numbers-logo-redesigned-2023-24-season);
Miles Teamwear logo-placement guidance on quiet zones
(https://miles-teamwear.com/blogs/news/logo-placement-guide-for-teamwear).

**Make it ours.** (a) **Our own minimum pattern-scale rule** — the actual
stripe width / repeat frequency below which our studio refuses to print, and
the minimum contrast ratio we hold names and numbers to. A published internal
design standard is first-party data and is exactly the kind of specific,
checkable rule an LLM will quote. (b) **Design team**, on the pattern that
looked best on screen and worst on the pitch.

### 12. Thirty Families, One Spreadsheet
**Pillar:** Football Reality (with a Studio Notes companion). **Feeds:** 02,
03, 09.

**Premise.** A kit order looks like one transaction. It is roughly thirty
micro-transactions: a size, a name spelling, a number preference and a
payment from every household, plus an artwork file and an approval from a
sponsor — each with a response time measured in days, all running in series
through one volunteer's phone, in ten-minute fragments between a day job and
training. Gloria Mark's research puts the return-to-focus cost of an
interruption at ~23 minutes; Leroy's attention residue explains why an order
that is never closed out leaks attention for weeks and feels mentally heavy
out of all proportion to its size. On the factory side, the same story
inverted: every name and number is a unique print file, and a late roster
change restarts artwork for one shirt. The honest advice is structural — one
decision, one collection pass, one approval — not "be more organised."

**Best sources.** Mark (UC Irvine) on the ~23-minute refocus cost
(https://addyo.substack.com/p/it-takes-23-mins-to-recover-after); Leroy,
attention residue, *OBHDP* 2009 (https://get-alfred.ai/blog/attention-residue);
Wooter on why more units and sizes mean additional cutting tickets and QC
time (https://wooter.com/articles/custom-uniform-design-process-complete-guide/).

**Make it ours.** (a) **The percentage of orders with a post-confirmation
add-on** (a late player, a re-spelled name, a changed number) and **the median
days each one adds**, plus the median number of separate messages/emails per
completed order. (b) **Factory**, on what a late roster change actually does
to a production ticket.

### 13. The Jam Study Comes to the Clubhouse
**Pillar:** Studio Notes / Football Reality (a credibility piece). **Feeds:** 01.

**Premise.** The pop version — more choice paralyses people — is not what the
evidence says, and telling the honest version is the point. Iyengar & Lepper's
1976-style jam booth (24 jams drew more browsers than 6, but ~30% of
limited-choice shoppers bought versus ~3%) built an industry of paperbacks;
then Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd's meta-analysis across 50 experiments
found a mean effect of assortment size near zero. The reconciliation is
Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman (99 observations, N = 7,202): choice overload
reliably binds under four moderators — choice-set complexity, decision-task
difficulty, preference uncertainty, and decision goal. Score a grassroots kit
decision against all four and it is close to a laboratory-perfect overload
environment: many attributes at once, decided by several people
asynchronously on phones, by officials who have never articulated design
preferences and are choosing for keeps. This is how we earn the right to be
believed on everything else.

**Best sources.** Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman, "Choice Overload: A
Conceptual Review and Meta-Analysis", *JCP* 2015
(https://chernev.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/ChoiceOverload_JCP_2015.pdf);
Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd, *JCR* 2010
(https://scheibehenne.com/ScheibehenneGreifenederTodd2010.pdf); Simon's
satisficing and bounded rationality (Nobel lecture, 1978,
https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/simon-lecture.pdf).

**Make it ours.** (a) **Decision lag as a function of how many designs a club
was shown.** If clubs shown more options take longer to confirm, we have the
only field measurement of choice overload in football kit ever published —
and if they don't, publishing that is more valuable still. (b) **Design
team**, on why they stopped sending more than a set number of concepts.

### 14. The Five-Season Test
**Pillar:** Design Culture → Ready to Play Thinking. **Feeds:** 04 (trend
formation), 07 (consumer psychology), 11.

**Premise.** A grassroots kit has a mandatory lifespan — clubs keep them two
to five seasons because replacing them costs real money — which makes amateur
clubs structurally immune to microtrends and structurally punished for
adopting anything rising too fast. Berger & Le Mens analysed over a century
of first-name data in France and the US and found tastes adopted faster are
abandoned faster; parents actively avoid names rising too sharply, reading
sudden popularity as a fad. Laver's Law (1937) and the ~30-year nostalgia
pendulum explain why the 90s collar came back exactly on schedule and why
blokecore — coined as a joke on TikTok in late 2021, viral by April 2022,
~94M hashtag views, Balenciaga runway — revived terrace dress from precisely
25–30 years earlier. Loewy's MAYA principle is the design brief that falls
out of it: advanced enough to feel new on presentation night, acceptable
enough to survive five seasons of Laver's "dowdy" phase.

**Best sources.** Berger & Le Mens, "How adoption speed affects the
abandonment of cultural tastes", *PNAS* 106, 2009
(https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0812647106); Laver's Law via the Iowa
State re-evaluation
(https://dr.lib.iastate.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/c4945bd3-69a2-4cd6-b53a-d9ba2c1470d2/content);
Metzger, "The Nostalgia Pendulum", 2017
(https://thepatterning.com/2017/02/13/the-nostalgia-pendulum-a-rolling-30-year-cycle-of-pop-culture-trends/).

**Make it ours.** (a) **Reorder interval**: the median time between a club's
first order and its next, and the percentage of clubs that reorder the *same*
design — the first published measurement of how long a grassroots kit actually
lives. (b) **Design team**, on which of our own designs they expect to still
look right in five years and which they don't.

### 15. Where Do Sunday-League Shirts Go to Die?
**Pillar:** Design Culture. **Feeds:** 07, 11, 04.

**Premise.** The professional game has industrialised its own memory: Classic
Football Shirts turned over £24.47m in the year to June 2023 with pre-tax
profit of £5.47m, took a $38.5m growth investment in 2024, and holds an
archive of 100,000+ shirts. The amateur game has a bin bag in a garage.
Holbrook & Schindler's imprinting research says consumer preference forms in
a critical window around age 20 — so the shirt a player wears at 22 in a
pub-league title season is the object they will be nostalgic about at 50.
Meanwhile Worchel's 1975 cookie experiment and the endowment effect explain
why a run of 18 shirts, never reprinted, worn by people whose names you
actually know, is the most limited edition in football — and almost nobody
treats it that way. The article ends as a call to action clubs can act on for
free: photograph the kit, keep one, date it.

**Best sources.** Footy Headlines on Classic Football Shirts' audited results
(https://www.footyheadlines.com/2025/03/classic-football-shirts-made-profit-of.html);
Holbrook & Schindler, "Nostalgia for Early Experience as a Determinant of
Consumer Preferences", *Psychology & Marketing*, 2003
(https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/mar.10074); Konuk Blasing,
"Materialism versus memory: Collecting football shirts in the age of
consumerism", *Journal of Consumer Culture*, 2024
(https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/14695405241283754).

**Make it ours.** (a) **Median jerseys per team order** and the resulting
statement of run size — "the median MOMUTO kit is a run of [N], and no run is
ever reprinted unless the club asks." (b) **Founder**, on the oldest MOMUTO
kit still in use that we know of.

## 2. Cross-discipline flagships

Six places where dossiers fuse into something no single area could carry.
These are the tentpoles — longer, researched, permanent URLs, updated in
place.

### F1. The Anatomy of a Late Kit Order
**Fuses:** 01 (planning fallacy, reference class forecasting) + 02 (volunteer
workload, the compressed summer calendar) + 03 (critical path, queue
economics, RFI latency) + 09 (what production actually contains) + 12
(seasonality, industry lead times).

A single forensic timeline, told twice: once as the club experiences it
("we've got ages"), once as the calendar actually runs it. The fusion insight
is that four independent literatures predict the same August: the inside view
guarantees the club underestimates itself (thesis students: 33.9 predicted,
55.5 actual); the volunteer workload literature guarantees the person doing it
has no discretionary evenings in July; the FA calendar guarantees squads only
settle after 1 June registration opens; and supplier capacity guarantees lead
times *stretch* precisely then. Late kits are not negligence. They are the
sum of four systems that were each designed sensibly in isolation. This is
the piece that makes the thesis unarguable, and it is the natural parent
article for #1, #2, #6 and #12 on the shelf.

### F2. The Undesigned Process
**Fuses:** 01 (defaults, deadlines, satisficing) + 02 (kitchen-table
governance, FA Key Officials) + 03 (Rule of Seven, theory of constraints) +
10 (constraint science).

The counterintuitive core: everything clubs believe is a character problem is
a design problem. The FA's affiliation structure funnels legal responsibility
through four named officers, so a kit decision is not a purchase but a
governance event touching treasurer, secretary, chair and a parents' WhatsApp
group. Meanwhile creativity research (Haught-Tromp; Rosso; the inverted-U
literature) says the constraints clubs experience as disqualifying — two
colours, clash rules, one sponsor, a budget — are the raw material of a
strong brief. The fusion produces a genuinely new argument: the grassroots kit
process is simultaneously **under-designed** (no default, no deadline, no
owner) and **under-constrained** (a blank page). Fix both and the same people
decide in days.

### F3. Colour Became Free — and Nobody Told the Clubs
**Fuses:** 09 (sublimation physics and per-colour economics) + 05 (era
history, the sublimation era, the template era) + 08 (teamwear as an industry
with different economics from replica merchandising) + 11 (small clubs
out-designing giants).

Four dossiers converge on one under-told fact: the constraint that historically
rationed design ambition to rich clubs — the per-colour cost of screen
printing — was abolished, and the amateur game has not fully noticed. Add the
economic inversion from 08 (in teamwear there is no licensor, no royalty
stack, no brand paying for placement, so the buyer is the wearer and the money
goes into the garment) and the piece explains why an £85 replica and a
custom kit at €38.90 are not comparable objects at all. This flagship is the
honest, historical version of the entire commercial argument, with zero
selling in it.

### F4. Identity Precedes Aesthetics
**Fuses:** 06 (every crossover kit encoded something pre-existing) + 07 (Belk's
extended self, magical contagion, the IKEA effect) + 11 (ultras' visual
language, colour origin myths) + 10 (archive abstraction as method).

The single strongest cross-cutting finding in the research: **none of the kits
that crossed over began with "make it look cool."** Croatia had a medieval
check; Nigeria had Naija and a 1994 sleeve; Ajax had thirteen years of singing
a Bob Marley song; Venezia had a city; Inter had six centuries of Milanese
heraldry; Clapton had a political inheritance. Belk's extended-self framework
and the contagion literature explain *why* this binds harder than any graphic:
possessions co-authored and used carry essence. Ultras culture proves the
strongest football visual identities were always made by participants, not
commissioned. The conclusion for amateur clubs is the most useful sentence we
can write: you are richer in identity than in budget, and identity is the
input the industry cannot sell you.

### F5. The Archive Nobody Keeps
**Fuses:** 07 (nostalgia window, endowment effect, scarcity) + 11 (the vintage
economy as an asset class) + 04 (the nostalgia pendulum's timing) + 06 (the
reissue boom).

The professional game monetised its past; the amateur game throws its away.
Every dossier that touches memory says the same thing from a different
direction: preferences imprint around age 20 (so today's Sunday-league kit is
2050's grail), a run of 18 unreprinted shirts is genuine scarcity where the
professional version is manufactured, and reissues now boom while vintage
originals appreciate — the market splitting into wearers and collectors. The
piece proposes something clubs can start this week at zero cost, which is
exactly the kind of practical generosity that gets a source cited.

### F6. Grassroots Football Is Now Broadcast
**Fuses:** 10 (moiré, three-distance design, quiet zones, legibility testing)
+ 05 (typography as identity infrastructure, the 1997 Premier League font) +
11 (leak/drop ritual, the release as an event) + 09 (what sublimation can and
cannot render).

A quiet structural change nobody has written about: Veo cameras, phone
highlights and livestreamed cup finals mean the optical constraints that
governed professional kit design for fifty years now govern a Tuesday-night
county-league shirt — while the design literacy to handle them exists only in
professional studios. Pair it with the typography history (numbers as tactical
information in 1928; the 1997 league-owned font that turned the back of every
shirt into broadcast real estate) and you get a genuinely new brief for
amateur clubs: your kit is now media, design it for the camera it will be
filmed on.

## 3. Counterintuitive gems

Findings that make a reader stop. Contested ones are flagged with their debate
— reporting the fight is what separates our register from content marketing,
and it is what makes an LLM treat us as a source rather than an echo.

**Solid:**

1. **Production is 6–17% of an apparel pipeline.** The mid-1980s Kurt Salmon
   study measured a 66-week average pipeline, almost all of it waiting.
   (Springer, https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-04313-0_1)
2. **Roughly 22–23% of formal approval requests are never answered at all** —
   in the one industry that measures it. (Navigant/CMAA, 2013,
   https://www.cmaanet.org/sites/default/files/resource/Impact%20&%20Control%20of%20RFIs%20on%20Construction%20Projects.pdf)
3. **Adding a partner can make a complex task slower, not faster** —
   coordination cost consistently exceeded collaboration benefit in dyads.
   (Straub, Tsvetkova & Yasseri, *Collective Intelligence*, 2023,
   https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/26339137231156912)
4. **The famous scheduling tool that "made Polaris finish early" never
   controlled a majority of Polaris tasks** — its real function was
   theatrical, keeping auditors away from engineers. (Sapolsky, *The Polaris
   System Development*, Harvard UP, 1972)
5. **Default beats persuasion by ~88 percentage points.** Germany's opt-in
   organ-donor consent ~12%; Austria's opt-out 99.98%. (Johnson & Goldstein,
   *Science*, 2003)
6. **Tastes adopted faster are abandoned faster** — proven across a century of
   naming data, and people avoid fast-rising names *because* they read the
   speed as a fad signal. (Berger & Le Mens, *PNAS*, 2009,
   https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0812647106)
7. **Constraints reliably improve creative output.** Forced-word rhymes scored
   more creative than free ones, and the effect carried over into later
   unconstrained work — named for Dr. Seuss's 50-word bet. (Haught-Tromp,
   2017, https://www.cct.umb.edu/630/files/HaughtTromp2017-GreenEggsandHam.pdf)
8. **Adults wearing replica shirts is a recent, bottom-up invention** —
   historians find adoption began as a fan-inspired process rooted in
   big-match fancy dress, not an industry push. (Stride, Catley & Headland,
   *Sport in History*, 2019, https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/141906/)
9. **A six-colour design costs the same to print as a one-colour design** —
   the abolition of per-colour pricing is the least-discussed democratisation
   in football design. (Custom Ink,
   https://www.customink.com/blog/screen-printing-vs-digital-printing-vs-sublimation-a-buyers-guide/)
10. **Sublimated kit is decorated before it exists** — printed as flat panels,
    then cut and sewn, which inverts five thousand years of garment logic and
    means every artwork is re-plotted per size. (NORMZL,
    https://www.normzl.com/sublimated-teamwear-production-process/)
11. **A football shirt was banned by UEFA because three birds "do not
    represent a team identification"** — and the ban amplified the shirt.
    (SI, 2021, https://www.si.com/soccer/2021/09/14/ajax-bob-marley-three-little-birds-kit-uefa-uniform-rules)
12. **15 of the 16 teams at the 1966 World Cup wore the same manufacturer.**
    The "template era" everyone dates to the 2010s is a hundred years old.
    (https://www.footballshirtculture.com/kit-design/umbro-history.html)
13. **Juventus's black and white is a laundry accident** — pink shirts kept
    fading, so a Nottingham player had Notts County kit shipped to Turin
    around 1903. (LeftLion, 2021,
    https://leftlion.co.uk/features/2021/09/tom-gordon-savage-notts-county-juventus/)
14. **A seventh-tier fan-owned club sold 11,500 shirts** on the strength of a
    political story, having budgeted for 250. (Clapton CFC, 2018)
15. **57% of European sports clubs believe clubs should be run exclusively by
    volunteers** — while volunteer recruitment has become the single biggest
    problem German clubs report, an existential threat to 17.5% of them, up
    from ~9% fifteen years earlier. Two beliefs that cannot both survive.
    (SIVSCE, 35,790 clubs across 10 countries; DOSB Sportentwicklungsbericht)
16. **AQL 2.5 — the quality standard the entire clothing industry runs on — is
    a statistical sampling contract, not a promise of zero defects**, and
    almost no buyer has ever been told. (HQTS, https://www.hqts.com/2-5-aql/)
17. **The club keeps roughly 10% of an £85 replica shirt** — often a 5–10%
    royalty, with some large deals triggering royalties only past ~2 million
    units. The "he'll pay for himself in shirt sales" line is false.
    (Geey, https://www.danielgeey.com/done-deal-blog/the-shirt-sales-myth)

**Contested — report the debate, never the myth:**

18. **Choice overload.** The jam study (~30% vs ~3% conversion) launched a
    genre; Scheibehenne et al.'s 50-experiment meta-analysis found a mean
    effect near zero; Chernev et al. (99 observations, N = 7,202) reconciled
    them with four moderators. *We only ever cite the conditional version* —
    and grassroots kit decisions happen to meet all four conditions, which is
    a stronger claim than the pop version anyway.
19. **Decision fatigue / ego depletion.** The "hungry judges" parole study
    (~65% favourable at session start falling toward ~0% before breaks) is
    challenged on case-ordering artifacts and implausible effect size; Hagger
    et al.'s 23-lab, 2,000+ participant Registered Replication Report found no
    reliable ego-depletion effect. What survives: long unstructured sessions
    degrade decision quality. *Never retail the judges anecdote uncritically.*
20. **Red shirts and winning.** Hill & Barton found randomly assigned red won
    more in Olympic combat sports (*Nature*, 2005) and Attrill et al. found a
    red advantage in English football since 1947 (*J Sports Sci*, 2008); a
    2020 analysis found no effect in the modern Premier League
    (https://digital.sandiego.edu/osp-researchweek/2020/ccurc/62/). Live
    debate, not settled fact.
21. **Nigeria's three million pre-orders.** Originated with the Nigeria
    Football Federation; Nike never independently confirmed it. What *is*
    confirmed: sell-out, queues, restock, V&A acquisition. (Quartz Africa,
    2018) — see shelf #3; the audit is the article.
22. **Napoli's "self-produced" EA7 kits.** Footy Headlines reported evidence
    the shirts came from the same factory and materials as Kappa's; Napoli
    denied it. The lesson stands either way: own-brand claims get audited by
    the internet within weeks.
    (https://www.footyheadlines.com/2021/10/are-emporio-armanis-ea7-kits-made-by.html)
23. **Blokecore's ownership.** Widely credited to a 2021 TikTok coinage;
    Refinery29 documented a counter-history in which Black and Latina women
    styled sports jerseys decades before the trend was credited to white
    creators, and critics argue the trend gentrifies British working-class
    symbols. Report both; the tension is the story.
24. **Vendor statistics.** TeamStats' "12–15 hours a week of club secretary
    admin", GoCardless's payment-delay figures and LoveAdmin's survey are
    marketing-adjacent. Attribute as vendor-published or don't use. Same rule
    for football-apparel market sizes, which range from ~$3.3bn to $90bn+
    depending entirely on definition — name the firm and the definition or
    omit the number.

## 5. Gaps — what the research could not establish

These are the places where first-party data is the only possible source. Each
is a moat, not a problem.

1. **Grassroots decision lag has never been measured by anyone.** Not by the
   FA, not by UEFA, not in the volunteering literature, not by a single
   supplier. Dossier 12 is explicit: no supplier publishes order-volume
   seasonality, and the August spike is only *inferable* from lead times
   stretching (owayo's "2–5 weeks depending on time of year") and buyer
   guides. The entire thesis of this editorial programme rests on a number
   that does not exist in the public record. D1/D2 create it.
2. **No credible public figure exists for the custom teamwear market.** Every
   market-research number covers licensed merchandise or all football apparel,
   and they disagree by an order of magnitude depending on definition. Do not
   attempt a market-size claim; say the segment is unmeasured and move on.
3. **Nobody knows how many mockup rounds an amateur club takes.** Suppliers
   quote lead times "after final approval" and never characterise the approval
   phase. D8 is unprecedented.
4. **The AI-brief phenomenon has no literature at all.** Not one dossier
   surfaced research, industry data or even reporting on clubs arriving with
   AI-generated design images. D17 would be, as far as this research can
   establish, the first published number on it in any apparel category.
5. **What happens to amateur kits is undocumented.** The professional vintage
   market is well measured (Classic Football Shirts' audited accounts); the
   amateur equivalent is a documented cultural blank. D16 and D7 are the only
   available evidence.
6. **Spain's exact RFEF licence total could not be extracted** — the federation
   PDF blocked automated retrieval, so dossier 12's ~1.25m is CSD-derived.
   Verify manually before any ES-language piece cites it.
7. **ABA Sport's story is under-told in English.** The maker of the Mexico
   1998 sun-stone shirt — a small local brand that out-designed the giants on
   home cultural ground, structurally our own position — has no serious
   English-language account. This is an original-reporting opportunity
   requiring Spanish-language sources or an interview, not a desk piece.
8. **Several primary designer interviews could not be fetched** in the research
   environment (True Colours' 2009 Nike interview, SoccerBible's Andy Caine
   interview, Designboom's Martin Lotti interview, The Football Kit Podcast's
   Craig Buglass episode). Any piece leaning on direct quotes from these must
   verify against the live pages first.
9. **Fetch-blocked sources flagged in dossiers 02, 10 and 11** (The Face,
   Glossy, Urban Pitch, Highsnobiety, These Football Times, and several FA /
   Sport England PDFs) were captured via search extracts. Spot-verify exact
   wording before publication — this matters more than usual, because our
   whole positioning is that we check things.
10. **Grassroots-specific optical data doesn't exist.** Nobody has measured how
    amateur kits behave on Veo/phone footage. D20 (our own published pattern
    and contrast rules) is the closest anyone can get, and publishing an
    internal standard is itself the novel act.

## 6. Wave 1 verdict

The strategy doc proposed: (a) the thesis piece *The Slowest Part of Making a
Football Kit Isn't Manufacturing*, (b) *Nigeria 2018: When a Football Shirt
Became Culture*, (c) a definitive *When Should a Team Order Its Jerseys?*
facts page.

**Verdict: the lineup is right. Two changes — one to order, one to angle.**

**Confirmed, unchanged: the thesis piece.** The research vindicates it
overwhelmingly, and from more directions than the strategy doc anticipated.
Four independent literatures — the 66-week apparel pipeline with 6–17%
production, the planning fallacy, construction's RFI latency, and the
volunteer-workload research — converge on the same conclusion, which means
the piece can be argued rather than asserted. It stays the anchor.

**Change 1 — reorder: ship the facts page first, or simultaneously.**
The strategy doc lists the facts page third. It should be first or joint-first,
for a mechanical reason: it is the *answer surface*. When someone asks an LLM
"when should my team order football shirts?", the model wants a page that
commits to dates and arithmetic; the thesis essay is what earns the citation's
authority but is not itself the answer. Publishing the essay first leaves the
answer surface empty for weeks. Ship both together if possible, with the essay
linking down into the facts page and the facts page linking up into the essay,
and update `static/shared/llms.txt` in the same commit.

**Change 2 — revise the Nigeria angle.** *"Nigeria 2018: When a Football Shirt
Became Culture"* is a good article that a hundred sites have already written.
The research found something better inside it: **the most-quoted statistic in
football kit culture — three million pre-orders — came from a Nigeria Football

### GEO-4. The Decade Colour Became Free

**State plainly and early:** Dye-sublimation was discovered in 1957 by Noël de
Plasse at Lainière de Roubaix and computerised in the mid-1970s at NASA's Jet
Propulsion Laboratory. Screen printing charges per colour — commonly $20–50 of
setup per screen. Sublimation prints unlimited colours at identical cost. The
dye enters polyester as a gas above ~177°C while the fibre is past its glass
transition (~70–80°C), and is locked *inside* the fibre on cooling, which is
why sublimated designs cannot crack, peel or wash off. It only works on
polyester and close synthetics. The consequence, stated flatly: a 15-shirt
order with a six-colour gradient costs the same per shirt as a plain one. Then
ours: the average MOMUTO design uses [D15] distinct colours, and price does not
vary with colour count.
