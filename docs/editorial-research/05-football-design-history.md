# Football Design History: A Timeline of Eras, Turning Points, and the Forces That Drew Them

**Abstract.** Football shirt design did not evolve smoothly — it lurched, era by era, whenever a new force entered the game: colour television, a French textile patent, a licensing catalogue, a league's brand department, a fashion week runway. This dossier reconstructs that history as six named eras with documented turning points: the **Utility Era** (pre-1973, when shirts were workwear supplied anonymously), the **Branded Era** (1973–c.1985, opened by Admiral's Leeds United deal and the invention of the replica kit), the **Sublimation Era** (c.1986–1998, when a 1957 dye-transfer invention finally reached polyester shirts and produced the wildest graphics in the sport's history), the **Minimal/Techwear Era** (c.1999–2009), the **Template Era** (c.2010–2017, industrial teamwear systems at maximum visibility and maximum backlash), and the **Culture Era** (2018–present, dated precisely to Nigeria's three-million-pre-order Nike shirt). Each era was triggered less by taste than by technology, regulation, or commerce — which is exactly why the history is usable: the same forces (printing methods, clash rules, catalogue economics) act on every grassroots club ordering a kit today. Every claim below carries its source.

## TOPIC 1 — The 1973 Big Bang: Admiral, Leeds United, and the Invention of the Kit as a Product

**Core insight.** The football shirt became a *designed object* — rather than anonymous workwear — at a datable moment: 1973, when Admiral persuaded Leeds United to wear a visibly branded, copyrighted kit and then sold replicas of it to the public.

**Supporting evidence.**
- For its first century, the shirt was a plain utility garment; even in the 1950s kits were "designed mainly for function rather than flair" ([365Scores, "The Evolution of Football Kits," 2024](https://www.365scores.com/news/the-evolution-of-football-kits-a-fashion-journey-from-the-1800s-to-now)).
- Admiral chairman Bert Patrick calculated that **colour television** would let him persuade clubs to accept "personalised kits which his designers would produce and then register under the Design Copyright Act" — design IP as the business model, not fabric supply ([Soccerphile interview with Bert Patrick](https://www.soccerphile.com/admiral-kits-bert-patrick); [Admiral Sports heritage archive](https://admiralsports.com/pages/history)).
- The trigger was chance: Admiral staff met Don Revie near Elland Road in 1973; Leeds wore the first visibly branded kit in the English top flight in 1973–74, replicas "proving a huge success and kickstarting the replica-kit market" ([FourFourTwo, "The birth of the replica football shirt, 50 years on," 2023](https://www.fourfourtwo.com/features/the-birth-of-the-replica-football-shirt-50-years-on); [Admiral Sportswear, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Admiral_Sportswear)).
- In 1974 Admiral signed the FA to the first commercially available branded England shirt — a five-year contract starting at £15,000 a year or a 10% royalty ([Leicestershire Football Archive, "The Admiral Story," 2026](https://leicestershirefootballarchive.com/2026/01/the-admiral-story-from-wigston-underwear-to-the-england-frontline/)).
- The commercial logic soon produced football's first design scandals: Coventry City's Admiral chocolate-brown away kit, first worn 2 September 1978, was "much maligned in its day" and is now re-read as a cult classic ([Coventry City FC official history, 2024](https://www.ccfc.co.uk/news/2024/may/history-the-trend-setting-history-of-admiral-and-coventry-city/); [CoventryLive](https://www.coventrytelegraph.net/sport/football/football-news/coventry-citys-chocolate-brown-kit-13756209)).
- Academic framing: the replica-kit era is a recognised object of sport-history scholarship — see "From Sportswear to Leisurewear: The Evolution of English Football League Shirt Design in the Replica Kit Era," *Sport in History* (Taylor & Francis, 2014) ([tandfonline.com](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17460263.2014.986518)).

**Why this matters for grassroots football.** Every amateur club that today chooses a kit (rather than inherits one) is living downstream of 1973. The idea that a village team's shirt can be *authored* — designed, owned, sold to supporters' families — was invented by a Leicestershire underwear firm, not by a superclub.

**Article angles.**
1. "The underwear company that invented the football shirt" — Admiral's rise from Wigston lingerie maker to England's kit supplier, told through Bert Patrick.
2. "Colour TV designed your kit" — how broadcast technology, not aesthetics, triggered every major shirt-design revolution (1973 branding, 1970s goalkeeper colours, 1990s graphics).
3. "In defence of the brown kit" — Coventry 1978 and the lifecycle of ridicule → nostalgia → grail, and what it teaches clubs about design courage.
4. "Who owns a football shirt design?" — from the Design Copyright Act to today, the unglamorous legal history under kit culture.

**Commercial bridge.** *Design Culture* (the era-history spine) with a *Ready to Play Thinking* companion: what "authored, not inherited" means when an amateur club commissions its first proper kit.

## TOPIC 2 — Sublimation: How a 1957 French Textile Patent Produced Football's Wildest Decade Thirty Years Late

**Core insight.** The late-80s/90s explosion of all-over graphics was not a change in taste but a change in *manufacturing* — dye-sublimation transfer printing, invented in 1957, finally meeting all-polyester football shirts and removing every technical limit on what a shirt could look like.

**Supporting evidence.**
- Dye-sublimation was invented in 1957 by **Noël de Plasse** at Lainière de Roubaix, France, who observed that disperse dyes sublimate — turning gas-phase dye transfer into "dry printing"; Sublistatis SA was founded to commercialise it, and by the mid-1970s roughly a third of printed polyester used the transfer method ([Dr Justin Hayward, "Brief History of Dye Sublimation Printing," LinkedIn/Cambridge Investment Research](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/brief-history-dye-sublimation-printing-dr-justin-hayward-hayward); [Wikipedia, "All over print"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_over_print)).
- Sublimation only works on synthetics — so it could not touch football until cotton gave way to polyester in the 1970s–80s ([Arbish Sports, "The History of Sublimation Designing"](https://arbishsports.com/sublimation-designing-evolution-and-future/); [FAN/FARE, "Decades of Decadence"](https://fan-fare.co.uk/blogs/news/decades-of-decadence-a-brief-history-of-classic-football-shirts)).
- The pivot shirt is **Denmark's Hummel 1986** home: half pinstripes, half tonal red stripes, chevron sleeves — "the insanity of late-80s/early-90s shirt design is usually regarded as starting with the Holland '88 shirt, but the seeds were sown two years before" ([The Football Attic, "50 Greatest Football Shirts Ever #4," 2015](http://50gfse.blogspot.com/2015/08/4-denmark-1986-87-home-shirt-by-hummel.html); [Vice, "How Denmark's '86 World Cup Shirt Became a Cult Classic"](https://www.vice.com/en/article/how-denmarks-86-world-cup-shirt-became-a-cult-classic/); [nss sports, "Template History: Hummel Denmark 1986"](https://www.nss-sports.com/en/lifestyle/31497/template-history-hummel-denmark-1986)).
- Full flowering: sublimation "provided designers with the ability to achieve vibrant all-over graphics," e.g. the tie-dyed USA 1994 away; mid-90s, Umbro and Kappa pushed further with **woven jacquard** fabrics carrying complex designs directly in the weave ([Bodega, "Three Lions and Three Thousand Trapezoids: A Guide to the Unrestrained World of '90s Soccer Shirts"](https://bdgastore.com/blogs/blogdega/three-lions-and-three-thousand-trapezoids-a-guide-to-the-unrestrained-world-of-90s-soccer-shirts)).
- The 1990s remain the single most collected and celebrated shirt decade — the era new colours entered club palettes and "boundaries were pushed further" ([Classic Football Shirts blog, "1990s Soccer Jerseys' Most Iconic Designs"](https://classicfootballshirts.se/blog/1990s-soccer-jerseys-most-iconic-designs/)).

**Why this matters for grassroots football.** Sublimation is now the *default* manufacturing method for custom amateur kits — the technology that once made Denmark '86 avant-garde is what makes a 30-shirt order for a Sunday-league club economically possible at all. Grassroots clubs today have fewer design constraints than Serie A had in 1985.

**Article angles.**
1. "The 30-year fuse" — a technology invented in a Roubaix wool mill in 1957 detonates at Mexico '86: the definitive history of sublimation in football.
2. "Denmark '86: the shirt that broke the rules a decade early" — anatomy of one design, from divided opinion to international design awards.
3. "Why the 90s looked like that" — an explanation piece: every 'crazy 90s shirt' listicle exists because of one printing process; the taste followed the machine.
4. "Your Sunday league kit is more advanced than the 1994 World Cup" — what full-sublimation ordering means for amateur clubs, technically and creatively.

**Commercial bridge.** *Studio Notes* — MOMUTO manufactures with the direct descendant of this process; documenting how sublimation actually works (and what it can't do) is credible first-person material.

## TOPIC 3 — Templates: From Umbro's 1966 Monopoly to the 2010s Backlash

**Core insight.** The tension that defines kit aesthetics — industrial scale versus club identity — is as old as the industry itself: 15 of 16 teams at the 1966 World Cup wore one manufacturer, and the 2010s "template era" was simply that logic made visible and finally rejected.

**Supporting evidence.**
- Umbro, founded by the Humphreys brothers in Wilmslow (1920, renamed Umbro 1924), supplied Manchester City's 1934 FA Cup winners and Brazil's 1958/62/70 World Cup winners; at the **1966 World Cup, 15 of the 16 finalists wore Umbro** — everyone but the USSR ([Football Shirt Culture, "The History of Umbro"](https://www.footballshirtculture.com/kit-design/umbro-history.html); [Footy Boots, "Umbro History: Timeline and Facts"](https://www.footy-boots.com/umbro-world-cup-1966-owen-shearer/); [Umbro, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umbro)).
- The modern teamwear catalogue is a formal, public system: adidas publishes named template generations (Condivo, Tiro, Tabela, Campeon, Squadra…) in downloadable catalogues, each defining what thousands of clubs at every level will wear ([Kitbliss teamwear catalogue archive](https://kitbliss.co.nz/data/teamwear/adidas-catalogues.html); [Footy Headlines, adidas teamwear coverage](https://www.footyheadlines.com/2015/03/adidas-2015-16-teamwear-kit-templates.html)).
- Peak template, peak backlash: at the 2018 World Cup the adidas Condivo 18 template served as the away kit for Algeria, Belgium, Northern Ireland, Scotland, Sweden and Wales; adidas had earlier issued near-identical shirts to Armenia, the Faroe Islands, Luxembourg and San Marino; Egypt's federation was publicly criticised for accepting a generic design ([Footy Headlines, "Dozens of Adidas' 2018-19 Kits Feature Same Design," 2018](https://www.footyheadlines.com/2018/03/all-same-adidas-away-kits-share-design.html); [KingFut, "Egypt deserves a better 2018 World Cup kit design," 2017](https://www.kingfut.com/2017/11/27/feature-adidas-negligence-egyptian-national-team-kit/); [ESPN, on Puma's template shirts](https://global.espn.com/football/blog-the-toe-poke/story/4733455/world-cup-kits-argentinagermanymexico-jerseys-are-hits-but-too-many-of-pumas-template-shirts-miss-the-mark)).
- The template is not inherently the villain: some of the most loved designs in history *are* templates — Hummel's 1986 chevron system was worn across nations and clubs, and Urban Pitch has catalogued "the greatest World Cup kit templates of all time" ([nss sports, "Template History: Hummel Denmark 1986"](https://www.nss-sports.com/en/lifestyle/31497/template-history-hummel-denmark-1986); [Urban Pitch](https://urbanpitch.com/the-greatest-world-cup-kit-templates-of-all-time/)).
- The 2020s counter-move: manufacturers now design 90s-referencing templates on purpose — e.g. the adidas Condivo 20 with a "massive collar" and abstract all-over print "inspired by the 1990s" ([Footy Headlines, 2019](https://www.footyheadlines.com/2019/05/adidas-condivo-20-template-2020-21-season.html?m=1)).

**Why this matters for grassroots football.** Amateur clubs live almost entirely inside the template economy — the catalogue is usually their only option. Understanding templates as a 100-year-old system (with good and bad generations) reframes the grassroots question from "which catalogue page?" to "how much identity can we put into the system we're given — or do we step outside it?"

**Article angles.**
1. "Everyone wore Umbro: the 1966 World Cup as the first template tournament" — the monopoly nobody remembers, and what it did to design.
2. "A taxonomy of the template" — Condivo, Tiro, Tabela: reading 40 years of teamwear catalogues as an archaeological record of what football thought clubs deserved.
3. "The template backlash of 2018" — six nations, one shirt: the moment fans decided identity was non-negotiable, with Egypt as the case study.
4. "In praise of the good template" — Hummel '86 and why systems can carry identity better than one-offs when the system itself has a point of view.

**Commercial bridge.** *Ready to Play Thinking* — this is MOMUTO's core argument territory: the documented difference between catalogue teamwear and authored custom kits, told through history rather than sales copy.

## TOPIC 4 — Typography: How Letters and Numbers on a Shirt Became Identity Infrastructure

**Core insight.** Shirt typography evolved in three distinct regimes — numbers as *positional information* (1928), names-and-numbers as *player identity* (1990s), and standardised league fonts as *brand identity* (1997) — and the third regime turned the back of every shirt into licensed media space.

**Supporting evidence.**
- On **25 August 1928**, Arsenal (away at Sheffield Wednesday) and Chelsea (home to Swansea Town) became the first English teams to wear numbered shirts, numbers 1–11 mapped to positions in the 2–3–5 formation ([This Day in Football History, "25 August 1928: Football By Numbers"](http://tdifh.blogspot.com/2013/08/25-august-1928-football-by-numbers.html); [Old School Football, "Evolution of the football shirt number"](https://oldschoolfootball.co.uk/blogs/news/evolution-of-the-football-shirt-number)).
- When the Premier League launched in 1992 there was **no standard font** — each club's typeface came from its kit manufacturer (Umbro, Asics, adidas, Nike all supplied their own) ([FourFourTwo, "Every Premier League badge and font EVER"](https://www.fourfourtwo.com/features/every-premier-league-badge-and-font-ever)).
- In **1997** the Premier League imposed a single league-wide serif typeface, "inspired by the blocky American football fonts of the 90s"; since then "every time a shot of a player's back appeared on screen, in print, or on social media, the brand has been visible," with fonts cycled every six or ten years ([nss sports, "What it means to have a universal font like in the Premier League"](https://www.nss-sports.com/en/lifestyle/32701/font-premier-league); [FourFourTwo, ibid.](https://www.fourfourtwo.com/features/every-premier-league-badge-and-font-ever)).
- The 1997–2007 lettering became so iconic that Avery Dennison — the Premier League's nameset supplier since 2019 — reissued it to original specifications as a retro product ([Images Magazine, "Avery Dennison brings back retro font for Premier League shirts"](https://www.images-magazine.com/avery-dennison-retro-font-premier-league-shirts/)).
- A whole collector economy now exists around period-correct "namesets," organised by font era (e.g. "EPL 1997–2007") ([House of Namesets](https://www.houseofnamesets.co.uk/collections/epl-1997-2007)).

**Why this matters for grassroots football.** Typography is the cheapest identity lever an amateur club has — and the least considered. The professional game's lesson is that a consistent, owned letterform repeated hundreds of times a season builds recognition faster than any crest redesign; grassroots clubs usually accept whatever block font the printer defaults to.

**Article angles.**
1. "1–11: when numbers were tactics" — the 1928 experiment and how positional numbering encoded an entire (now-dead) way of playing.
2. "The font is the brand" — 1997 and the invention of the league-owned typeface; why the Premier League understood the back of the shirt was broadcast real estate before anyone else.
3. "Nameset archaeology" — the collector subculture that dates shirts by their letters, and what it says about typography as memory.
4. "Choosing a font for a Sunday club" — a practical design essay applying the 1997 lesson at amateur scale.

**Commercial bridge.** *Design Culture* for the history; *Studio Notes* for a companion piece on how MOMUTO sets names and numbers (legibility at distance, sublimated vs heat-pressed).

## TOPIC 5 — Rules as Designers: How IFAB Law 4 and Clash Regulations Quietly Authored the Kit Canon

**Core insight.** Some of football design's most productive genres — the away kit, the goalkeeper shirt, the third kit — were not invented by designers at all; they are compliance artefacts of colour-clash regulation, and the history of those rules is a hidden history of the sport's aesthetics.

**Supporting evidence.**
- IFAB's Law 4 today requires only that the two teams "wear colours that distinguish them from each other and the match officials," and that each goalkeeper be distinguishable from all outfield players — colour is the one aesthetic dimension the Laws actually govern ([IFAB, Law 4 — The Players' Equipment](https://www.theifab.com/laws/latest/the-players-equipment/); [IFAB, Player's kit](https://www.footballrules.com/components-equipment/players-kit/)).
- Goalkeeper colour was tightly rationed for half a century: an IFAB ruling of **1921** limited keepers to green, blue, scarlet and white (yellow/black for internationals), and green dominated by default; the palette was only relaxed in the **1970s** — a shift that coincided with colour television, when keepers like Peter Bonetti and Phil Parkes started wearing red ([Goalkeepers Are Different, "Getting Shirty: a history of goalkeeper kits"](https://www.goalkeepersaredifferent.com/keepers/getting-shirty.html)).
- The Laws even contain an aesthetic escape valve: if both goalkeepers' shirts are the same colour and neither has a spare, the referee simply plays the match ([IFAB, Law 4](https://www.theifab.com/laws/latest/the-players-equipment/)).
- Above IFAB, competition bodies layer detailed kit codes — UEFA's Kit Regulations run to dozens of pages governing contrast, advertising space, and approval procedures ([UEFA Kit Regulations, 2008 edition, PDF](https://www.uefa.com/multimediafiles/download/regulations/uefa/others/72/77/76/727776_download.pdf)).
- The commercial world learned to exploit the regulatory seams: Coventry's Talbot-era designs "blatantly incorporated the logo of their sponsors" into the kit design itself, testing where identity ended and advertising began ([Old Football Shirts, Coventry City archive](https://www.oldfootballshirts.com/en/teams/c/coventry-city/old-coventry-city-football-shirts-t27.html); [CCFC official history](https://www.ccfc.co.uk/news/2024/may/history-the-trend-setting-history-of-admiral-and-coventry-city/)).

**Why this matters for grassroots football.** Amateur leagues enforce the same clash logic with less infrastructure — a Sunday club with one kit and no spare bibs experiences Law 4 more brutally than Real Madrid ever will. Designing a grassroots kit *starts* with the regulatory question (what do the other twelve teams in the division wear?), which professional design discourse almost never mentions.

**Article angles.**
1. "The away kit doesn't exist because anyone wanted it" — a genre history of the change strip as pure regulatory by-product that became a design playground.
2. "Why goalkeepers wore green for fifty years" — the 1921 IFAB ruling, colour TV, and the slow liberation of the loudest shirt on the pitch.
3. "Read the league handbook before you open the mood board" — clash-mapping a real amateur division as a design exercise.
4. "The referee will allow it" — the strangest edge cases in Law 4 and what they reveal about football's pragmatism on aesthetics.

**Commercial bridge.** *Football Reality* — documenting the actual constraints amateur clubs face (clash rules, one-kit budgets, colour approval) is exactly the ground-truth reporting that pillar exists for.

## TOPIC 6 — The Culture Era: Crest Minimalism, Nigeria 2018, and the Shirt's Escape from the Stadium

**Core insight.** Since roughly 2017 the football shirt's primary audience has ceased to be the matchday crowd: crests were redrawn for app icons, kits are launched at fashion weeks, and the era's founding event — Nigeria 2018, with over three million pre-orders — proved a shirt could out-sell its team's entire footballing relevance.

**Supporting evidence.**
- **Juventus, 2017**: the club replaced its heraldic shield with a bare "J" monogram, designed with Interbrand under president Andrea Agnelli, explicitly "to move beyond the boundaries of football" and scale down to jersey sleeves and mobile-app icons ([Juventus official announcement, "New logo, new era," 2017](https://www.juventus.com/en/news/articles/new-logo-new-era); [The Week, "Juventus badge: the biggest redesign in football history?"](https://theweek.com/80657/juventus-badge-the-biggest-redesign-in-football-history)). **Inter Milan followed in 2021** with the streamlined "I M" crest, stated to make the club "a cultural brand as well as a sporting one" ([CBS Sports, 2021](https://www.cbssports.com/soccer/news/inter-milan-unveil-new-logo-as-part-of-i-m-rebrand)).
- **Nigeria 2018** (designed for Nike by Matthew Wolff, referencing the 1994 Super Eagles shirt): the NFF reported **over three million pre-orders**; the shirt sold out in an hour on Oxford Street and in minutes online, and immediately resold at multiples of its $90 price — a record for an African federation, against Manchester United's 2.8 million shirts sold in all of 2016 ([Quartz Africa, 2018](https://qz.com/africa/1290171/nigeria-nike-world-cup-soccer-jersey-pre-order-sales-top-three-million); [CNBC, 2018](https://www.cnbc.com/2018/06/22/nikes-nigerian-world-cup-jersey-breaks-sales-records.html); [FourFourTwo, 2018](https://www.fourfourtwo.com/features/nike-receive-over-three-million-orders-nigerias-world-cup-kit-which-isnt-even-out-yet)). The shirt is now in the **V&A's permanent collection** ([V&A collections, Wolff/Nike Nigeria kit](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O1575826/football-kit-t-shirt-wolff-matthew)).
- The fashion crossover became systemic: Palace x Juventus (2019) as the grail that "blurred the lines between sportswear, streetwear and fashion," football shirts on Balenciaga's FW20 runway, Gucci x Palace (2022) ([Culted, "How the football kit infiltrated the world of fashion"](https://culted.com/fashion/how-the-football-kit-infiltrated-the-world-of-fashion); [VIVRANT, "Culture FC"](https://www.vivrant.co.uk/home/culture-fc)); **Grace Wales Bonner's Jamaica kit for adidas debuted at Paris Fashion Week** ([Urban Pitch](https://urbanpitch.com/adidas-wales-bonner-and-jamaica-the-trend-of-meshing-football-with-high-fashion/)); "blokecore" carried vintage shirts onto TikTok as everyday fashion ([Culted, ibid.](https://culted.com/fashion/how-the-football-kit-infiltrated-the-world-of-fashion)).
- The institutions ratified the shift: London's Design Museum staged the sport's first major design exhibition, *Football: Designing the Beautiful Game* (8 April – 28 August 2022, with the National Football Museum; 500+ objects across Performance, Identity, Crowds, Spectacle, Play) ([Design Museum](https://designmuseum.org/exhibitions/football-designing-the-beautiful-game); [Design Week, 2022](https://www.designweek.co.uk/issues/4-10-april-2022/football-design-exhibition/)). Neal Heard's *The Football Shirts Book: The Connoisseur's Guide* (Ebury, 2017) gave the collector canon its reference text ([Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/33790790-the-football-shirts-book)).
- The backlash is part of the era: minimalist crest redesigns are contested as erasure of heraldic memory ([Abdoulie Lemon, "The Rise of Minimalism in Football," Medium](https://abdoulielemon19.medium.com/the-rise-of-minimalism-in-football-a-journey-towards-plainness-bffe21a1844f); [Rabbit Logo on the Juventus crest](https://rabbitlogo.com/blog/juventus-logo/)).

**Why this matters for grassroots football.** The Culture Era collapsed the prestige gap: a shirt's cultural value now comes from its design story, not its league. A well-told amateur kit — rooted in place, heritage, and a real community — can operate by exactly the rules that made Nigeria 2018 and Venezia's Kappa-era shirts objects of desire ([Football Kit Archive, Venezia FC kit history](https://www.footballkitarchive.com/venezia-fc-kits-t530/)).

**Article angles.**
1. "The three-million-shirt team that went out in the group stage" — Nigeria 2018 as the founding document of the Culture Era, from Wolff's sketch to the V&A vitrine.
2. "The crest wars" — Juventus's J and Inter's I M: what is lost and gained when a hundred years of heraldry is redrawn for a 32-pixel app icon; includes the grassroots version of the question.
3. "When the shirt left the stadium" — a cultural history of blokecore, Palace x Juve, and Wales Bonner: how the terrace uniform became runway material.
4. "Museum-grade" — what it means that football kit is now curated by the Design Museum and the V&A, and who gets left out of the canon.

**Commercial bridge.** *Design Culture* — this is the era MOMUTO's whole editorial identity ("we document how football design evolves, then turn those ideas into kits amateur clubs can wear") is native to; the grassroots-canon question in angle 4 doubles as *Football Reality* material.

## Working Timeline of Named Eras (for reuse across articles)

| Era | Dates | Turning point | Driving force |
|---|---|---|---|
| Utility Era | pre-1973 | — | Function; anonymous supply (Umbro's quiet monopoly, 1966: 15/16 teams) |
| Branded Era | 1973–c.1985 | Admiral x Leeds United, 1973 | Colour TV, design copyright, replica market |
| Sublimation Era | c.1986–1998 | Denmark Hummel '86 | Polyester + dye-sublimation (invented 1957) + jacquard weaves |
| Minimal/Techwear Era | c.1999–2009 | reaction to 90s excess | Performance-fabric marketing, pared-back palettes |
| Template Era | c.2010–2017 | Condivo-class teamwear catalogues at World Cup visibility | Industrial scale; backlash peaks 2018 |
| Culture Era | 2018–present | Nigeria x Nike, 3M pre-orders | Fashion, identity, digital-first crests, collector culture |
