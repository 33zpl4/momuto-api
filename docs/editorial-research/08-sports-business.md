# Sports Business: The Money Machine Behind the Football Shirt

**Editorial research dossier — MOMUTO | Area: Sports Business | Compiled August 2026**

Football's shirt economy is really three separate businesses wearing the same garment: a sponsorship business (brands paying clubs nine-figure guarantees for visibility), a licensing business (manufacturers and retailers keeping 80–90% of every replica shirt sold), and a teamwear business (clubs and teams paying to dress themselves). The first two dominate headlines — Real Madrid's €1.1bn adidas deal, £85 average Premier League replica prices — but they are economically inverted from what fans assume: clubs typically pocket less than 10% of a replica shirt's price, because kit deals are guaranteed-fee media buys, not merchandise partnerships. The third business, teamwear, is the one MOMUTO operates in: a fragmented, fast-digitising segment where the buyer wears the product, nobody pays a licensing royalty, and the "sponsor" is the local chip shop. This dossier documents the numbers, the history (from the Jägermeister loophole of 1973 to the gambling shirt ban of 2026), and the editorial angles hiding inside them.

---

## Topic 1 — The Shirt Sales Myth: clubs keep almost none of the shirt money

**Core insight:** The widely believed idea that a star signing "pays for himself in shirt sales" is false — kit manufacturers and retailers capture roughly 80–90% of replica revenue, while clubs are paid guaranteed sponsorship fees plus modest royalties that often only trigger above high sales thresholds.

**Supporting evidence:**

- Sports lawyer Daniel Geey ("The Shirt Sales Myth"): clubs receive large up-front payments from kit manufacturers rather than per-shirt income; royalty payments in many large deals only kick in after a high sales threshold (e.g. ~2 million shirts) has been passed. (https://www.danielgeey.com/done-deal-blog/the-shirt-sales-myth)
- Typical anatomy of a ~£75 shirt sale: manufacturer ~60%, retailer ~20%, VAT ~10%, club ~10% — and often the club's slice is a 5–10% royalty, sometimes structured as ~7.5% commission on top of the fixed fee. (The Football Week, 2025: https://thefootballweek.com/2025/04/09/the-business-model-of-football-kits-and-merchandise-inside-the-billion-dollar-game/; Sports Business Institute Barcelona: https://www.sbibarcelona.com/football-business/football-kits-the-business-behind/)
- The exception that proves the rule: Liverpool's 2020 Nike deal had a *low* guarantee — £30m/year, versus £45m/year from New Balance — but 20% royalties on net sales of licensed products (5% on footwear), targeting £60–70m/year total. New Balance sued in the High Court in 2019 over its matching clause and lost, partly because it could not match Nike's distribution and marketing reach ("superstar athletes and influencers" — LeBron James, Serena Williams, Drake). (SportsPro: https://www.sportspro.com/news/liverpool-nike-kit-deal-value-new-balance-royalties/; Sky Sports: https://www.skysports.com/football/news/11669/11838589/liverpools-multi-million-pound-new-balance-sponsorship-dispute-reaches-high-court; case summary, Northridge Law, 2019: https://northridgelaw.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/Case-Summary-New-Balance-v-Liverpool-Football-Club.pdf)

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** The professional shirt economy is a licensing pyramid in which the wearer (the fan) subsidises everyone above them. Grassroots kit economics are the exact inverse: the buyer is the wearer, there is no licensor, no royalty stack, and no brand paying for placement — so an amateur club's £300–600 kit budget buys fabric, printing and logistics, not brand rent. Understanding the pyramid explains why a replica shirt costs £85 and a full custom kit for one player can cost less than half that.

1. "Your new signing will pay for himself in shirt sales" — anatomy of football's most durable financial myth, and who actually banks the £85.
2. Follow one shirt's £85 from till to balance sheet: a forensic breakdown of manufacturer, retailer, taxman and club shares.
3. The Liverpool v New Balance trial as the moment kit deals were revealed in open court — what the documents showed about how these contracts really work.
4. Why the club crest on a replica shirt is essentially a rented billboard — and what a shirt looks like when nobody is charging rent.

**Commercial bridge:** Football Reality — MOMUTO's segment is the one place in football where 100% of the shirt price goes into the shirt.

## Topic 2 — Kit deal hyperinflation: from £60m a decade to €1.1bn a contract

**Core insight:** Elite kit-supply deals have inflated roughly 15-fold in 30 years — Manchester United's per-year kit income went from ~£10m (Umbro, 1996) to £90m (adidas, 2025) — because manufacturers are no longer buying shirt sales, they are buying global marketing real estate.

**Supporting evidence:**

- Manchester United timeline: Umbro extension 1996 ≈ £60m over six years; Nike 2002 world record £302.9m over 13 years (~£23.5m/year); adidas 2015 £750m over 10 years (~£75m/year — BBC News, 2014: https://feeds.bbci.co.uk/news/business-28282444); adidas extension 2023 £900m over 10 years from 2025. (Deal history: https://www.salaryleaks.com/blogs/manchester-united-kit-deals; https://www.unitedkits.com/articles/manufacturers.php)
- Real Madrid–adidas, announced 2026: eight-year extension guaranteeing €1.1bn (~$139m/year), the most lucrative kit deal in football history, edging past United's adidas terms. adidas has supplied Real Madrid continuously since 1998. (Sportico, 2026: https://www.sportico.com/business/sponsorship/2026/real-madrid-adidas-jersey-kit-sponsorship-1234902875/; SportsPro: https://www.sportspro.com/news/real-madrid-confirm-adidas-kit-extension/)
- Barcelona–Nike, November 2024: renewal reported at over €1.7bn total running to 2038 — struck only after Barcelona publicly explored self-manufacturing and a Puma bid forced Nike to improve terms. (SportsPro, 2024: https://www.sportspro.com/news/barcelona-nike-kit-supplier-sponsorship-deal-value-2038-november-2024/; SoccerBible on the self-manufacture threat: https://www.soccerbible.com/news/2024/03/barcelona-to-manufacture-its-own-kits/)
- Context: Deloitte Football Money League 2026 — the top 20 clubs generated a record €12.4bn in 2024/25; commercial revenue (€5.3bn, 43% of total) was the largest revenue stream for the third year running. Real Madrid alone: €1,161m revenue, €594m of it commercial. (Deloitte: https://www.deloitte.com/uk/en/services/consulting-financial/analysis/deloitte-football-money-league.html; Goal summary: https://www.goal.com/en/news/deloitte-football-money-league-2026/bltedf9c4b411ec7212)

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** These deals concentrate manufacturer marketing budgets on perhaps 30 clubs worldwide. Everyone below that line — the other 99.99% of football — is a customer, not a partner. The inflation at the top is precisely why the big brands' grassroots offer is a catalogue of standard templates: elite exclusivity is the product; grassroots is the afterthought that pays for it.

**Article angles:**

1. From £60m a decade to €1.1bn a contract: charting 30 years of kit-deal hyperinflation in one dataset (United's five contracts as the throughline).
2. What adidas is actually buying for €138m a year — hint: it isn't shirt sales.
3. The Barcelona bluff: what happened when the world's most famous club threatened to make its own kit — and why it still couldn't.
4. Football's velvet rope: why kit brands pay 30 clubs and charge everyone else.

**Commercial bridge:** Football Reality — the economics that decide which clubs get bespoke design and which get page 47 of a teamwear catalogue.

## Topic 3 — The shirt front: fifty years from banned to banned again

**Core insight:** Shirt-front sponsorship — football's single most visible commercial invention — has come full circle in fifty years: smuggled in through a German loophole in 1973, banned by the English FA in 1976, legalised in 1977, hidden from BBC cameras into the 1980s, inflated into an £8–12m-per-club market, and now partially re-banned as the Premier League removes gambling brands from shirt fronts in 2026/27.

**Supporting evidence:**

- 1973: Jägermeister's Günter Mast circumvents DFB rules by making the brand's stag logo Eintracht Braunschweig's *club crest*; the DFB approves shirt advertising later that year. (These Football Times, 2020: https://thesefootballtimes.co/2020/05/27/how-jagermeister-changed-the-world-of-corporate-shirt-sponsorships/; SPORTFIVE: https://sportfive.com/beyond-the-match/insights/the-history-of-shirt-sponsoring-in-the-bundesliga)
- 24 January 1976: Southern League Kettering Town become the first British club to wear a sponsor ("Kettering Tyres") — the FA orders it removed; after lobbying backed by Bolton and Derby, the ban is lifted in June 1977. (Vice: https://www.vice.com/en/article/the-unlikely-pioneers-of-shirt-sponsorship-in-english-football/; Northampton Museums: https://timeline.northamptonmuseums.com/timeline/kettering-town-football-club-shirt)
- 1979: Liverpool sign English football's first top-flight shirt deal with Hitachi — but broadcasting rules mean sponsor logos cannot be shown in televised matches for the first years of the deal. (Liverpool FC official history: https://www.liverpoolfc.com/news/history-liverpools-shirt-sponsors; Play Up Liverpool: https://playupliverpool.com/1979/07/25/liverpool-sign-deal-with-hitachi/)
- 2026/27: the Premier League's voluntary front-of-shirt gambling ban takes effect. Estimated collective shortfall ~£80m; mid-table clubs that previously commanded £8–12m/season report replacement offers at roughly half that; multiple clubs entered the season without a front-of-shirt sponsor. (Gambling Insider: https://www.gamblinginsider.com/news/152495/premier-league-gambling-ban-shirt-sponsors-80m-loss; Sky Sports, 2023 decision: https://www.skysports.com/football/news/11095/12856367/premier-league-clubs-agree-to-withdraw-gambling-sponsorships-on-front-of-shirts; sector breakdown: https://www.scoreandchange.com/overview-of-the-2026-2027-premier-league-sponsors/)

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** Grassroots shirts have always worked the way Kettering intended — a local business pays a few hundred pounds, gets its name on twelve chests, and underwrites the season. The professional game industrialised that gesture into a market now large enough to require government-adjacent regulation. The amateur version remains the original, honest form: sponsorship as community patronage, not media buying. (Cost benchmark: sponsoring a grassroots team's kit typically runs in the low hundreds of pounds — Gift of Kit: https://giftofkit.com/news/cost-of-sponsoring-a-grassroots-team/)

**Article angles:**

1. The stag on the shirt: how a schnapps magnate's crest-swap loophole invented the sponsored football shirt.
2. Kettering Tyres to crypto exchanges: fifty years of the shirt front as a mirror of the economy (tyres → electronics → lager → payday loans → gambling → finance).
3. Banned, legalised, hidden from the BBC, banned again: the shirt front as football's longest-running regulatory battle.
4. The £300 chip-shop sponsorship: why the grassroots shirt deal still works exactly like 1976 — and why that's the purest form of the idea.

**Commercial bridge:** Design Culture — the sponsor patch as a design object with a fifty-year social history, from stag crest to Sunday-league chip shop.

## Topic 4 — The price of fandom: replica inflation and the affordability revolt

**Core insight:** The average Premier League replica shirt now costs £85 — with prices at most clubs rising faster than clothing inflation — and the result is a measurable affordability crisis: half of fans say they can't afford the new shirt and a quarter buy counterfeits.

**Supporting evidence:**

- Average Premier League replica price reached £85 in 2025; a study of 78 clubs found shirt prices up 34.9% over the past decade, with 59 of 78 clubs raising prices faster than CPI clothing inflation. (Reporting via inkl/local press: https://www.inkl.com/news/football-fans-face-paying-up-to-ps90-for-replica-shirts; price guide context: https://www.mystershirt.com/en-us/blogs/are-football-shirts-a-good-investment/soccer-jerseys-price)
- Half of Premier League fans report being unable to afford their team's new shirt; 25% buy counterfeit shirts from unofficial websites (23% for their children), with consumer groups warning of "blatant greed." (AOL/PA reporting: https://www.aol.com/articles/half-premier-league-fans-priced-094014000.html; Berkeley Economic Review on the counterfeit trade: https://econreview.studentorg.berkeley.edu/real-fans-fake-shirts-the-underground-trade-of-counterfeit-jerseys/)
- The tiering machine: "authentic"/match-spec shirts sell $50–100 above replicas (top authentics ~$170 vs replicas ~$90–105) — a deliberate good-better-best ladder that pushes the ceiling price ever higher. (Mystershirt price guide, above; TribalFootball on cost structure: https://www.tribalfootball.com/article/soccer-premier-league-uncovered-why-are-football-replica-shirts-so-pricey-c75b4de7-eec3-4469-926d-ae50b8f8f1eb)
- Market backdrop: licensed sports merchandise is a ~$38bn global market (2025), with football the largest sport at roughly 31–41% of demand and apparel ~35% of product mix. (Grand View Research: https://www.grandviewresearch.com/industry-analysis/licensed-sports-merchandise-market-report; Market.us: https://market.us/report/licensed-sports-merchandise-market/) Note for editorial use: football-apparel market-size estimates from research vendors range from ~$3.3bn to ~$90bn+ depending on definition (jerseys vs all football sportswear) — treat any single headline number with suspicion and always state the definition. (Compare IMARC https://www.imarcgroup.com/football-sportswear-market vs Market Research Future https://www.marketresearchfuture.com/reports/football-apparel-market-24460 vs Market.us https://market.us/report/soccer-sportswear-market/)

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** The affordability revolt reframes what "value" means in football apparel. An £85 polyester replica carries perhaps £50+ of brand, licence and retail margin; a custom grassroots shirt of comparable technical spec carries almost none. As replica pricing detaches from fans' incomes, the cultural prestige of the *worn-for-real* shirt — your own team, your own name, your own sponsor — is a genuine counter-story, not a consolation prize.

**Article angles:**

1. The £85 question: what you're actually paying for in a replica shirt (spoiler: not the fabric).
2. Real fans, fake shirts: the counterfeit boom as a rational consumer response to licensing economics.
3. "Authentic" vs "replica": how the two-tier shirt turned fandom into a loyalty means test.
4. The market that can't measure itself: why football-apparel market sizes range from $3bn to $90bn, and what that says about research-vendor numbers in sport.

**Commercial bridge:** Ready to Play Thinking — value defined by what the garment does on a pitch, not by the licence printed on it.

## Topic 5 — Teamwear: the football apparel industry's invisible second half

**Core insight:** Teamwear — kit bought by the teams who wear it — is a structurally different industry from replica merchandising (no licences, no royalties, direct-to-club economics), and it is being reshaped by digital mass customisation: 3D configurators and sublimation printing now give amateur clubs design freedom that used to be exclusive to professionals.

**Supporting evidence:**

- Distinct economics and players: the specialist teamwear tier is dominated not by Nike/adidas but by Joma, Macron, Kappa, Errea, Umbro, Hummel, Jako, Uhlsport, O'Neills and others, sold largely through catalogues and regional distributors (e.g. Premier Teamwear's multi-brand kit catalogues: https://footballkits.co/kit-catalogues/). Joma has grown past €200m in revenue while deliberately refusing to enter lifestyle apparel — team sport only — calling the constraint a competitive asset. (Verified Market Reports, Official Football Kits Market: https://www.verifiedmarketreports.com/product/official-football-kits-market/)
- Digital disruption: spized (Germany, founded 2019 by ex-handball pro Robin Teppich) built a direct-to-club model around a real-time 3D configurator and sublimation printing aimed at grassroots clubs; in 2026 Hummel partnered with spized for custom teamwear — an established brand outsourcing customisation to a digital native. (SGI Europe, 2026: https://www.sgieurope.com/brands/hummel-partners-with-spized-for-custom-teamwear/121767.article; owayo, made-in-Germany 3D-configured custom kit: https://www.owayo.com/)
- The demand side is enormous and under-counted: in England alone, 15.7 million people play football (2024) and over a million volunteer in grassroots football; the FA values adult grassroots football's social and economic contribution at £15.9bn/year, up from £10.2bn in 2021. (The FA grassroots strategy 2024–28: https://www.thefa.com/news/2024/oct/28/a-new-landmark-strategy-20241028; FA report, "The Social and Economic Value of Adult Grassroots Football in England": https://www.thefa.com/-/media/thefacom-new/files/about-the-fa/research-and-insight-team/the-social-and-economic-value-of-adult-grassroots-football-in-england.ashx)
- Unit economics of the customer: outfitting a full amateur team costs roughly £250–600 in kit, inside season budgets where pitch hire alone runs £500–1,000 — kit is a meaningful, recurring, price-sensitive purchase. (Pendle Sportswear: https://www.pendlesportswear.co.uk/blog/cost-of-running-grassroots-football-team/; The Soccer Store: https://www.thesoccerstore.co.uk/blog/latest-news/how-much-does-it-cost-to-set-up-a-grassroots-football-team/)
- Even the elite tier signals fragmentation: nine different kit brands supply the 20 Premier League clubs in 2025/26 (adidas 8, Nike just 3, plus Castore, Joma, Hummel, Macron, Puma, SUDU, Umbro) — the duopoly concentrates spend on a few flagships while challengers use top-flight visibility as a shop window for their teamwear businesses. (Kitgeek: https://kitgeek.co.uk/2025/07/15/kit-manufacturers-breakdown-2025-26/; Blinkfire Analytics: https://analyticsblog.blinkfire.com/blog/2025/08/27/2025-26-premier-league-kits-brands-and-sectors-shaping-this-season/)

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** This is the segment where grassroots clubs are the *primary market*, not a marketing afterthought. The technology shift (sublimation + web configurators) removed the minimum-order and template constraints that historically forced amateur clubs into catalogue kits, meaning a Sunday-league side can now commission a shirt as considered as a Serie A one. The competitive question in teamwear is no longer distribution muscle — it is design quality, service, and speed.

**Article angles:**

1. The invisible half of the football shirt industry: why nobody reports on the kits people actually play in.
2. Joma's €200m refusal: the teamwear specialist that got big by *not* chasing lifestyle fashion.
3. When Hummel called a startup: what an established brand outsourcing customisation to a 3D-configurator company says about where teamwear is going.
4. £15.9bn a year, zero column inches: grassroots football as England's most under-reported industry.
5. Sublimation changed everything: the printing technology that ended the era of the catalogue kit.

**Commercial bridge:** Studio Notes — MOMUTO's own segment documented as an industry: its history, its technology shift, and the design freedom it now offers amateur clubs.
