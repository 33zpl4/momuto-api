# Manufacturing & Materials: What Actually Happens Between "Order" and "Kickoff"

**Editorial research dossier — MOMUTO — Area 09: Manufacturing & Materials**
*Compiled August 2026. Sources cited inline and listed at the end.*

## Abstract

Custom teamwear sits on top of three quietly remarkable pieces of industrial history that almost nobody in football ever hears about: a 1957 French discovery that dye can jump from paper to fabric as a gas, a NASA-adjacent engineer who computerised that process in the 1970s, and a DuPont polymer from 1958 that gave sportswear its stretch — and that must now survive a 200°C press that sits well above its damage threshold. This dossier documents what is physically real in performance-fabric claims (the exercise-physiology literature is far more sceptical than the marketing), how dye-sublimation actually works at the molecular level and why it abolished the per-colour economics that once rationed design to the rich, why sublimated garments are built "inside out" compared with normal clothing (decorated before they exist), and what the 2–3 week production window in custom teamwear genuinely contains — including the statistical honesty of AQL quality control. The through-line: the most interesting stories in kit manufacturing are not about innovation announcements; they are about physics, constraints, and trade-offs that manufacturers rarely explain and customers never see.

## 1. Moisture wicking is real physics — but the marketing claims the wrong benefit

**Core insight:** Capillary wicking in polyester is genuine and measurable, but controlled studies show it does *not* keep an exercising body meaningfully cooler than cotton — its real, defensible benefits are comfort, drier skin contact, and faster post-exercise recovery, and whether a garment wicks at all depends on fibre *geometry* versus wash-off chemical finishes.

**Supporting evidence:**

- The mechanism is real: hydrophobic synthetic fibres move sweat by capillary action through the spaces between fibres, spreading it over a larger surface to evaporate faster than it would on skin ([REI Expert Advice, "What Does Moisture-Wicking Mean?"](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/what-does-moisture-wicking-mean.html); [Nike, "Moisture-Wicking Fabric, Explained"](https://www.nike.com/a/nike-what-is-moisture-wicking)).
- But the headline claim collapses under lab conditions. Sperlich, Born, Lefter & Holmberg (2013), *Wilderness & Environmental Medicine*, "Exercising in a Hot Environment: Which T-shirt to Wear?", put trained runners through submaximal and high-intensity running at ~30°C in cotton vs. polyester shirts: **no differences in skin, core, or body temperature between fibre types during exercise**. Where polyester won: lower relative humidity at chest and back, and lower body temperature and less shivering sensation *during recovery* ([PubMed 23870763](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23870763/); [SAGE full record](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1016/j.wem.2013.04.005)).
- An earlier study reached the same conclusion in its title: Gavin et al. (2001), "Clothing fabric does not affect thermoregulation during exercise in moderate heat" ([PubMed 11740309](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11740309/)).
- What *does* separate good from mediocre performance fabric is engineering, not slogans. Coolmax-type fibres are extruded with a four- or six-channel cross-section, increasing fibre surface area by roughly 20% and drying dramatically faster than cotton; because the wicking is built into the fibre's shape, it never washes out. Topical "wicking finishes", by contrast, are chemical coatings that degrade with laundering ([PCIAW, "COOLMAX EcoMade Fibre"](https://pciaw.org/coolmax-ecomade-fibre-cool-comfort-in-professional-clothing/); [DeadSoxy, "CoolMax Socks Explained"](https://deadsoxy.com/blogs/sock-knowledge-base/coolmax-socks-explained)).
- The everyday killer of wicking performance is domestic: fabric softener coats and blocks capillary channels — the most common reason performance garments stop wicking ([DeadSoxy](https://deadsoxy.com/blogs/sock-knowledge-base/coolmax-socks-explained)).

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** Amateur players are the biggest consumers of performance-fabric marketing and the least equipped to audit it. The honest story — "your shirt won't lower your core temperature, but it will stop clinging to you in the 70th minute and won't chill you on the touchline afterwards" — is more useful than the myth, and the care advice (never use fabric softener on kit) is genuinely actionable for the person who washes fifteen shirts every Sunday night.

**Article angles:**

1. "Your shirt is not air conditioning" — what sports science actually found when it raced cotton against polyester, and why the result surprised everyone including the researchers.
2. The secret life of a fibre cross-section: how wicking is a question of *shape*, not chemicals — and how to tell engineered fabric from sprayed-on marketing.
3. "The person who ruins more football kits than anyone else is holding a bottle of fabric softener" — a care-and-maintenance piece grounded in polymer science.
4. Why the real benefit of polyester shows up *after* the final whistle: the recovery-phase data nobody quotes.

**Commercial bridge:** *Ready to Play Thinking* — honest materials explainers build trust in a category saturated with pseudo-science; a kit-care guide is a natural evergreen asset.

## 2. Sublimation printing: a 1957 French accident, a space-lab computer, and dye that becomes gas

**Core insight:** The technology behind every modern custom football kit was discovered by a French textile chemist in 1957 who noticed dyes evaporating, and was computerised in the mid-1970s by an engineer at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory — and the physics (dye entering the fibre as a gas and being trapped inside it) is precisely why sublimated designs can never crack, peel, or wash off.

**Supporting evidence:**

- In 1957 Noël de Plasse, a chemist at Lainière de Roubaix in northern France, observed that certain dyes sublimate — pass directly from solid to gas — and realised this could transfer printed designs from paper to fabric with heat. The company Sublistatic SA was founded to commercialise the technique; by 1970 some 24 million m² of transfer paper had been produced, rising to roughly 350 million metres by the mid-1970s, when about a third of printed polyester was decorated by transfer ([Dr Justin Hayward, "Brief History of Dye Sublimation Printing", LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/brief-history-dye-sublimation-printing-dr-justin-hayward-hayward)).
- The digital leap came from space research: Wes Hoekstra, an engineer at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Pasadena, developed the first computer-driven sublimation imaging system in the mid-1970s (filing in 1972) and is credited as the "father of computer image sublimation" ([Wooter Apparel, "The History and Future of Sublimation Printing"](https://wooterapparel.com/blog/the-history-and-future-of-sublimation-printing); [Longforte, "The Evolution of Dye Sublimation Printing"](https://www.longforte.com/blogs/trending-topics/the-evolution-of-dye-sublimation-printing-a-journey-through-history)).
- The physics: disperse dyes printed onto transfer paper are pressed against polyester at roughly 190–210°C. The dye sublimates — solid to gas, skipping liquid entirely — at temperatures above ~177°C. Simultaneously, the heat takes polyester past its glass transition temperature (~70–80°C), loosening polymer chains and opening microscopic gaps in the fibre's amorphous regions. Gaseous dye diffuses into those gaps; on cooling, the structure closes and the dye is locked *inside* the fibre ([Textile Tuts, "Disperse Dyes"](https://textiletuts.com/disperse-dyes/); [P2InfoHouse, "Disperse Dyes and Their Application to Polyester"](https://p2infohouse.org/ref/31/30223.pdf); [Fujia, sublimation temperature reference](https://fujiaprinter.com/sublimation-temperature-chart/)).
- Consequence: there is no ink layer *on* the garment. The image and the fabric are the same object — which is why sublimated prints stretch with the fabric and cannot crack, peel, or flake the way surface prints do, and why the fabric's hand-feel is unchanged ([Blink Gallery, "Dye Sublimation Printing: The Facts"](https://blinkgalleryusa.com/dye-sublimation-printing-facts/)).
- The same physics is the technique's hard limit: it only works on polyester (and close synthetic cousins), because only those fibres have the right glass-transition behaviour, and it favours white/light base fabric because the process adds dye, not opaque pigment.

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** Every grassroots kit ordered today is a direct descendant of a Cold War-era French textile lab and a space-imaging workshop. The "printed inside the fibre" fact is also the honest answer to the most common customer question in teamwear — "will the design wash off?" — and explains why a sublimated shirt outlives a vinyl-numbered one.

**Article angles:**

1. "The dye that learned to fly": Noël de Plasse, Sublistatic, and how a 1957 observation in Roubaix ended up on a Sunday-league pitch.
2. From Mars imagery to matchday: the JPL engineer who accidentally became the godfather of custom sportswear.
3. "Your shirt isn't printed — it's dyed from the inside": a 500-word physics explainer with real numbers (200°C, solid-to-gas, glass transition) that answers "will it peel?" forever.
4. Why you can't sublimate a cotton T-shirt: the glass-transition story as an accessible piece of materials science.

## 3. Sublimation abolished the price of colour — and the 1990s kit explosion was the proof

**Core insight:** Screen printing charges by the colour (each colour needs its own physical screen and setup); sublimation collapsed the marginal cost of colour, gradient, and pattern to zero — an economic shift, first made visible in the anarchic goalkeeper shirts of the 1990s, that quietly ended the era when design complexity was a luxury only big clubs could afford.

**Supporting evidence:**

- Screen printing's economics are per-colour: each colour in a design requires its own screen, with setup commonly $20–50 per screen, which rewards high volume and few colours and penalises gradients and fine detail ([Custom Ink, "Screen Printing vs. Digital Printing vs. Sublimation"](https://www.customink.com/blog/screen-printing-vs-digital-printing-vs-sublimation-a-buyers-guide/)).
- Sublimation prints unlimited colours, gradients, and photographic imagery at identical cost, with full-garment coverage and no minimum-order pressure from setup costs ([RareCustom, "Sublimation vs. Screen Printing for Custom Jerseys"](https://rarecustom.com/blog/sublimation-vs-screen-printing-custom-jerseys); [Capra Sport comparison](https://caprasport.com/sublimation-vs-screen-printing-vs-heat-transfer/)).
- Football saw the consequences almost immediately. The 1990 World Cup opened an era in which goalkeeper kits went "from an afterthought to an outlandish mix of spots, stripes, polygons, prints, hoops, hashing and a lot more besides — sometimes all at once" ([ESPN, "Goalkeeper kit designs in the 1990s were on another level"](https://www.espn.com/soccer/story/_/id/38672402/goalkeeper-kit-designs-1990s-were-another-level)). Jorge Campos's self-designed neon kits and David Seaman's Euro '96 waves became icons precisely because manufacturers could suddenly print anything ([SoccerBible, "Bring It Back: The 'Ugly' 90s Goalkeeper Jersey"](https://www.soccerbible.com/news/2019/06/bring-it-back-the-ugly-90s-goalkeeper-jersey/)).
- Kit historians attribute the aesthetic directly to production technology: "the development of sublimation printing provided designers with the ability to achieve vibrant all-over graphics", alongside jacquard weaves knitted into the cloth ([VICE Football Store, "1990s Goalkeeper Shirts: The Golden Age of Football Kit Design"](https://vicefootballstore.com/blogs/blogs/were-goalkeeper-football-shirts-from-the-1990s-works-of-art)).
- The corollary for the amateur game: since setup costs are minimal, sublimation is *most* cost-efficient exactly where grassroots lives — small runs with many colours ([Custom Ink](https://www.customink.com/blog/screen-printing-vs-digital-printing-vs-sublimation-a-buyers-guide/)). A 15-shirt order with a six-colour gradient design costs the same per shirt as a plain one — economically unthinkable under screen printing.

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** For most of football history, design ambition scaled with budget: more colours meant more screens, more setup, more money. Sublimation inverted that. A pub team in 2026 can wear a design more complex than anything a First Division club could commission in 1985 — and pay nothing extra for the privilege. That is a genuine democratisation story hiding inside a printing technique.

**Article angles:**

1. "The decade colour became free": how one printing process explains why 90s kits went feral — and why the madness stopped when template economics returned.
2. Jorge Campos's neon shirts as an economics lecture: what per-colour pricing did to football aesthetics for a century, and what its abolition unleashed.
3. "Your Sunday-league team has more design freedom than Brazil had in 1970" — a provocation piece grounded in the cost tables.
4. Why gradients used to be a flex: reading old kits as artefacts of their production constraints.

## 4. The elastane paradox: pressing a heat-fragile fibre through a 200°C process

**Core insight:** The stretch in a polyester-elastane kit comes from a 1958 DuPont polymer that begins to degrade at temperatures far below the ~200°C sublimation press — so every stretch-fabric kit is the product of a tightly engineered truce (lower temperature, longer dwell, light pressure, pre-shrinking) that manufacturers almost never talk about.

**Supporting evidence:**

- Elastane (spandex/Lycra) was invented in 1958 by DuPont chemist Joseph Shivers — a polyether-polyurea copolymer that stretches to roughly five times its length and recovers, unlike rubber, while surviving sweat, lotions, and detergents ([National Inventors Hall of Fame, Joseph Shivers](https://www.invent.org/inductees/joseph-c-shivers-jr); [Smithsonian Magazine, "Thank(?) Joseph Shivers For Spandex"](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/thank-joseph-shivers-spandex-180967335/); [Wikipedia, "Spandex"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spandex)).
- Elastane is markedly heat-sensitive: prolonged exposure to high temperatures breaks down polyurethane chains, permanently reducing elastic recovery; care literature warns of permanent damage from hot washing and drying, and industry guidance notes spandex "can be changed or damaged by prolonged temperatures as low as 350°F" (~177°C) — below standard sublimation temperature ([T-Shirt Forums practitioner thread](https://www.t-shirtforums.com/threads/is-it-safe-to-heat-press-on-87-polyester-13-spandex.197188/); [Spandexbyyard, "Sublimation Printing on Spandex"](https://spandexbyyard.com/blogs/spandex-vs-other-stretch-fabrics-type-explained/sublimation-printing-on-spandex-perfsublimation-printing-on-spandex-perfect-stretch-printsect-stretch-prints)).
- The industry answer is a narrow process window: for elastane-rich polyester, recommended practice drops to roughly 180–190°C with adjusted dwell time and *light* pressure; blends with high spandex content (>20%) risk distortion and shrinkage under the press ([Spandexbyyard](https://spandexbyyard.com/blogs/spandex-vs-other-stretch-fabrics-type-explained/sublimation-printing-on-spandex-perfsublimation-printing-on-spandex-perfect-stretch-printsect-stretch-prints); [Sportek, "How to Prepare Polyester & Spandex Fabrics for Sublimation"](https://sportek.com/blogs/news/how-to-prepare-polyester-spandex-fabrics-for-sublimation-guideprepare-fabric-for-sublimation)).
- Get it wrong and the defects have names: "ghosting" (a blurred double image when fabric shifts or shrinks mid-transfer, mitigated by pre-shrinking the fabric before printing), scorching, and permanent loss of stretch ([Sportek](https://sportek.com/blogs/news/how-to-prepare-polyester-spandex-fabrics-for-sublimation-guideprepare-fabric-for-sublimation)).
- Done right, the payoff is unique: because the dye is inside the polyester component, the print stretches and recovers with the fabric with no cracking or peeling — something no surface decoration on stretch fabric can match ([Spandexbyyard, "Sublimation vs Screen Printing on Spandex"](https://spandexbyyard.com/blogs/spandex-vs-other-stretch-fabrics-type-explained/sublimation-vs-screen-printing-spandex)).
- Postscript for the kit bag: elastane's heat sensitivity continues after delivery — hot washes and tumble-dryers do to the fibre slowly what a careless press does quickly, with elastic performance degrading measurably over tens of wash cycles ([Alibaba Life Tips, spandex care science](https://lifetips.alibaba.com/laundry-secrets/care-for-spandex-lycra-elastane-clothes)).

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** "Polyester-elastane with full sublimation" sounds like a checkbox; it is actually a materials-engineering compromise performed on every panel of every shirt. This explains real things grassroots buyers notice — why stretch kits demand disciplined production, why cheap sublimated stretchwear sometimes arrives with blurred prints or baggy recovery, and why the club washing machine is the kit's second-biggest enemy.

**Article angles:**

1. "The fibre that hates the machine that prints it": the elastane-sublimation truce as an engineering story with real temperatures and failure modes.
2. Joseph Shivers and the accidental athletic revolution: how a fibre developed for girdles ended up defining how football kit fits.
3. A field guide to kit defects: ghosting, scorch, and dead stretch — what they look like, what went wrong at the press, and what a good factory does to prevent them.
4. "Your dryer is a slow-motion heat press": the wash-care piece, part two.

**Commercial bridge:** *Studio Notes* — process-honesty content; explaining the failure modes you engineer against is more credible than claiming perfection.

## 5. Sublimated kit is made inside out: decorated before the garment exists

**Core insight:** Unlike almost all other clothing, a sublimated football shirt is designed and printed as flat panels *before* it is cut and sewn — an inverted workflow that makes every design a piece of per-size engineering, moves the craft into seam-matching and panel alignment, and dictates the seam types (flatlock) that let the finished garment move with an athlete.

**Supporting evidence:**

- The sublimated-teamwear workflow runs: print the design onto transfer paper, transfer it onto flat fabric via heated calender, *then* precision-cut the printed panels against the garment pattern for each size, then sew — with printed panels "aligned carefully to maintain design consistency" across seams ([NORMZL, "Sublimated Teamwear Production: Process & Advantages"](https://www.normzl.com/sublimated-teamwear-production-process/); [Friend Brothers, "Custom Sportswear Manufacturing Process"](https://friendbrotherssports.com/custom-sports-uniforms-process/)).
- This is why sublimated garments can carry seam-to-seam, over-the-shoulder, edge-to-edge graphics that are impossible to apply to a finished garment — and also why misalignment at seams is the signature defect of a careless factory. Kit historians even note that the slight bleed and mismatch at 90s seams became part of that era's visual character ([VICE Football Store](https://vicefootballstore.com/blogs/blogs/were-goalkeeper-football-shirts-from-the-1990s-works-of-art)).
- Every artwork must be re-engineered per size: a stripe that hits the third rib on a size M must be re-plotted for XS and 3XL pattern pieces, which is part of what pre-production artwork time actually buys ([vPersonalize, custom apparel manufacturing at scale](https://vpersonalize.wordpress.com/)).
- Construction choices are athletic-movement choices. Flatlock stitching joins panels with no overlapping ridge, producing a flat seam that stretches and recovers with the fabric — the standard for garments worn against skin during exercise because it eliminates the chafing ridge of conventional seams ([ODs Designer Clothing, "Flatlock Seams Explained"](https://www.odsdesignerclothing.com/en-us/pages/glossary-flatlock-seams); [Prizzi, "What is a flatlock in Apparel Production"](https://prizzisewing.com/blogs/news/flatlock-in-apparel-production-2-top-of-the-line-sewing-machines)).
- Sleeve architecture is functional, not aesthetic: raglan sleeves (a diagonal seam from collarbone to underarm) remove the shoulder-point seam entirely, freeing overhead and rotational arm movement — which is why they dominate athletic tops ([Fighters Corner, "Flatlock vs Overlock Seams"](https://fighters-corner.com/blogs/fighters-guide/flatlock-vs-overlock-seams); [Ninghow, "Flatlock vs Overlock vs Coverstitch"](https://ninghow.com/blog/flatlock-vs-overlock-vs-coverstitch-seam-choices-for-activewear/)).

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** When a club uploads a design, they are not "printing a shirt" — they are commissioning a set of per-size engineering drawings. Understanding this explains why artwork approval takes days rather than minutes, why good factories obsess over seam matching, and gives grassroots buyers a concrete quality checklist: look at the seams (do stripes meet?), feel the seams (flat or ridged?), check the shoulder construction.

## 6. What the production window actually contains — and why quality is a statistical contract

**Core insight:** The 2–3 week production window in custom teamwear is not "printing time" — it is a chain of eight dependent stages (artwork engineering, approval, paper printing, calender transfer, cutting, sewing, finishing, inspection) in which the press occupies minutes per garment; and the industry's quality promise, AQL 2.5, is not "zero defects" but a formal statistical sampling contract most buyers have never heard of.

**Supporting evidence:**

- Industry-standard sequence for sublimated teamwear: design and artwork approval (typically 2–3 days, longer in peak season) → print-ready file preparation with per-size plotting → sublimation printing onto transfer paper → heat-transfer/calendering onto fabric → panel cutting to pattern → sewing and assembly → finishing → QC and packing ([Wooter, "Custom Uniform Design Process"](https://wooter.com/articles/custom-uniform-design-process-complete-guide/); [NORMZL](https://www.normzl.com/sublimated-teamwear-production-process/); [Friend Brothers](https://friendbrotherssports.com/custom-sports-uniforms-process/)).
- Industry-wide lead times are long: US-market fully sublimated uniforms commonly quote **20–25 business days in production** after final approval, roster submission, and payment, plus shipping; Australian guidance describes ~6 weeks as typical with extra time for design approvals ([Momentec Brands, FreeStyle Sublimation](https://www.momentecbrands.com/freestyle-sublimation); [SUB4, "How Long Does Custom Sport Teamwear Production Take in Australia?"](https://www.sub4apparel.com/blogs/news/how-long-does-custom-sport-teamwear-production-take-in-australia); [Ninghow, "How Long Does It Take To Manufacture Custom Team Clothing?"](https://ninghow.com/blog/how-long-does-it-take-to-manufacture-custom-team-clothing/)). Against that industry baseline, a 7–12 day production window (MOMUTO's stated range) sits at the fast end of the distribution — the editorial point is that *any* honest account of the window is differentiating, because most vendors publish a number without explaining what fills it.
- The bottleneck is rarely the press: sublimation transfer takes under a minute per panel set; the calendar time lives in artwork queues, approval loops, roster changes (every name/number is a unique print file), cutting tickets per size, and sewing capacity — which is why "more units and more sizes require additional cutting tickets and QC time" ([Wooter](https://wooter.com/articles/custom-uniform-design-process-complete-guide/); [Arcus Apparel Group, "Custom Clothing Production Timeline: Sample to Ship"](https://www.arcusag.com/custom-clothing-production-timeline-sample-to-ship/)).
- Quality control industry-wide runs on AQL (Acceptable Quality Limit) sampling, usually AQL 2.5 for apparel: inspectors draw a random sample sized by ISO 2859 tables, classify defects as critical (zero tolerance), major (≤2.5%), or minor (≤4%), and pass or fail the whole batch on the sample. It is explicitly "a sampling-based acceptance rule, not a guarantee of zero-defect goods" — the industry's formal mechanism for balancing speed against commercial risk ([HQTS, "Explaining AQL 2.5 for Quality Inspections"](https://www.hqts.com/2-5-aql/); [AQI Service, "What's AQL in Garment Inspection"](https://aqiservice.com/acceptable-quality-level-aql-in-garment-inspection/); [Textile Coach, AQL in the garment industry](https://www.textilecoach.net/post/aql-acceptable-quality-level-in-garment-industry)).

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** The number-one friction in grassroots kit buying is the wait, and the number-one mystery is what the wait is *for*. A transparent anatomy of the window — including the unglamorous truth that a late roster change restarts artwork for one shirt, and that quality is statistically sampled rather than magically perfect — converts frustration into understanding. Clubs plan seasons; honest lead-time literacy (order in June, not the week before the season) is genuinely useful service journalism.

**Article angles:**

1. "Where do the two weeks go?" — a day-by-day diary of one kit order moving through a factory, from artwork queue to courier scan.
2. The 45 seconds that matter: why the actual printing is the fastest part of making your kit, and what really sets the clock.
3. AQL 2.5: the quality contract the entire clothing industry runs on and nobody tells customers about — explained honestly.
4. "Why late roster changes cost more than you think": every name and number is a unique manufacturing event, not a checkout field.
