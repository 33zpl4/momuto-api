# Project Management First Principles: Why Kit Orders Run Out of Time

**Editorial research dossier 03 — MOMUTO**
**Researched: August 2026. All sources cited inline and in the closing list.**

## Abstract

A grassroots football kit order is a small project: a design decision, a collection of names and sizes, a sign-off, a purchase, a production run, a shipment. Seventy years of project management research — from the invention of the critical path method at DuPont in 1957 to Bent Flyvbjerg's databases of thousands of overrunning projects — converges on one finding that maps almost perfectly onto this timeline: the work is rarely the problem. Production time in the apparel industry has historically been as little as 6–17% of the total pipeline; the rest is waiting — for decisions, for approvals, for information, for someone to finally reply in the group chat. This dossier assembles the first-principles evidence (critical path method, theory of constraints, the planning fallacy, student syndrome, approval latency, queue economics, and the cost of context switching) and maps each principle onto the kit ordering timeline: design decision → logo/size collection → order → production → shipping. The through-line for editorial use: deadlines don't slip at the factory; they slip in the conversation before the factory ever hears about the order.

## 1. The critical path runs through people, not machines

**Core insight:** The critical path method was invented to schedule chemical plants, but its enduring lesson is that only a handful of tasks in any project actually determine the finish date — and in a kit order, every one of those tasks is a human decision, not a production step.

**Supporting evidence:**

- CPM was developed from December 1956 by James E. Kelley Jr. (Remington Rand) and Morgan R. Walker (DuPont) to schedule maintenance shutdowns of DuPont's chemical plants; first trials ran in 1957, the first paper appeared in 1959, and DuPont reported roughly 25% savings on shutdown time. Kelley's own retrospective is published by PMI: "Origins of CPM — a Personal History" ([PMI](https://www.pmi.org/learning/library/origins-cpm-personal-history-3762); see also [Mosaic Projects' history of CPM/PERT](https://mosaicprojects.com.au/PMKI-ZSY-030.php) and [historicprojects.com on Kelley & Walker 1957](https://www.historicprojects.com/Critical_Path.html)).
- The core mathematical insight: in any network of dependent tasks, one chain — the critical path — sets the minimum project duration. Accelerating anything off that chain buys nothing. Delaying anything on it delays everything.
- The rival method, PERT, was built in 1957–58 for the US Navy's Polaris missile program and became famous as the reason Polaris finished early. Political scientist Harvey Sapolsky's landmark study *The Polaris System Development* (Harvard University Press, 1972) demolished this: PERT never controlled even a majority of Polaris tasks; its real function was theatrical — impressive charts that kept Congress and auditors away from the engineers ([Sapolsky, Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Polaris_System_Development.html?id=h6LfAAAAMAAJ); [ResearchGate: "PERT, Polaris, and the realities of project execution"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/263572044_PERT_Polaris_and_the_realities_of_project_execution); [Eight to Late, "A PERT myth"](https://eight2late.com/2008/08/02/a-pert-myth/)).

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** Draw the network diagram of a kit order and the critical path is startling: "agree on a design" → "collect every player's size and initials" → "final sign-off" → order → production → shipping. Production (7–12 days) and shipping (10–15 days) are fixed-duration tasks with known durations and no committee attached. The variable-duration tasks — the ones that can take three days or three months — are all upstream, and all social. The Sapolsky finding adds a warning: tools and charts (a shared spreadsheet, a Trello board) don't shorten the critical path by existing; Polaris shows that scheduling artifacts can become performance rather than management.

**Article angles:**

1. "The chart that saved DuPont 25% — and what it says about your kit order": a history piece on Kelley & Walker that lands on the reader drawing the critical path of their own club's kit process and discovering production isn't on the slow part of it.
2. "The Polaris illusion": the missile program that finished early, the scheduling tool that took the credit, and why your club's planning spreadsheet might be theater too.
3. "Only three tasks matter": a practical explainer that walks a real kit timeline backwards from matchday one and identifies which tasks have float and which don't.

**Commercial bridge:** *Ready to Play Thinking* — a wall of finished designs deletes the longest variable-duration task ("agree on a design") from the critical path entirely, which is the only kind of acceleration CPM says counts.

## 2. The bottleneck is the decision (Goldratt in the group chat)

**Core insight:** Goldratt's theory of constraints says every system has one binding constraint and an hour lost there is an hour lost to the whole system — and in a kit order the constraint is not the sewing machine, it's the committee.

**Supporting evidence:**

- Eliyahu Goldratt, *The Goal* (1984): every system has at least one constraint; "an hour lost at a bottleneck is an hour lost out of the entire system," while "an hour saved at a non-bottleneck is worthless" ([Theory of Constraints Institute summary](https://www.tocinstitute.org/the-goal-summary.html); [PlanetTogether on TOC](https://www.planettogether.com/blog/goldratts-theory-of-constraints)). His five focusing steps begin with *identify* the constraint — most organisations optimise things that were never the constraint.
- Bain & Company research (Marcia Blenko, Michael Mankins, Paul Rogers, *Decide & Deliver*, 2010) quantified decision friction: the "Rule of Seven" — every person added to a decision group beyond seven reduces decision effectiveness by roughly 10%; taken to its logical conclusion, a group of 17 or more rarely decides anything ([Bain: "Effective decision making and the rule of 7"](https://www.bain.com/insights/effective-decision-making-and-the-rule-of-7/); [Bain: Decision-focused meetings](https://www.bain.com/insights/decision-insights-9-decision-focused-meetings/)).
- Applying TOC: speeding up a non-constraint (faster printing, express shipping) does nothing while the order sits un-placed. The corollary is brutal for kit timelines: a week lost in "which colour do we want?" is a week lost door-to-door, unrecoverable at any later stage.

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** A typical amateur club decides its kit in a WhatsApp group of 15–25 players plus a treasurer plus a sponsor — by Bain's arithmetic, a group constitutionally incapable of deciding. Meanwhile production and shipping are non-constraints with fixed capacity: no amount of urgency applied there recovers time lost at the true bottleneck. MOMUTO's own thesis ("teams don't fail because they lack ideas — they run out of time") is a restatement of Goldratt's first focusing step: the constraint has been misidentified as production when it is decision throughput.

**Article angles:**

1. "The Goal, but it's a Sunday league club": retelling Goldratt's factory novel with the kit committee as Herbie, the slow scout on the hike who sets the pace of the whole line.
2. "Twenty-two people, zero decisions": the Bain Rule of Seven applied to the club WhatsApp group — with a serious proposal for who should actually hold the kit decision (and it's one or two people).
3. "An hour lost at the bottleneck": why paying for express shipping on a late order is the definition of optimising a non-constraint.

**Commercial bridge:** *Football Reality* — documenting how clubs actually decide (group chats, straw polls, sponsor vetoes) is the constraint made visible; the editorial job is to name the bottleneck, not sell around it.

## 3. Deadlines slip by design: the planning fallacy and the student syndrome

**Core insight:** Fifty years of evidence shows humans systematically underestimate how long their own tasks will take and then start them as late as possible — so a kit ordered "in plenty of time" is, statistically, a kit ordered late.

**Supporting evidence:**

- Buehler, Griffin & Ross (1994), "Exploring the 'planning fallacy'" (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*): students predicted finishing their theses in 33.9 days on average; the actual average was 55.5 days — about 64% longer. Even when asked for an estimate they were *99% certain* of, only ~45% finished by it (and only ~30% by their best-guess date) ([Semantic Scholar PDF](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Exploring-the-%22planning-fallacy%22:-Why-people-their-Buehler-Griffin/f91964dad8c0e54cd58b1aa99e430b900fcf082b); [overview: Wikipedia, Planning fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planning_fallacy)). The concept originates with Kahneman & Tversky (1979).
- Bent Flyvbjerg's megaproject databases: nine out of ten megaprojects overrun their budgets; rail averages +45%, bridges/tunnels +34%, roads +20% (Flyvbjerg, Holm & Buhl 2002; ["Five things you should know about cost overrun"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0965856418309157); [reference class forecasting review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666721523000248)). Flyvbjerg & Budzier's HBR study of 1,471 IT projects (2011): average cost overrun 27%, but one in six projects was a "black swan" with ~200% cost overrun and ~70% schedule overrun ([HBR reprint PDF](https://www.alejandrobarros.com/wp-content/uploads/old/Black_Swan.pdf); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2229735)). His remedy — reference class forecasting — is to ignore your inside view and budget from what *similar past projects actually took*.
- Goldratt, *Critical Chain* (1997): people pad task estimates with hidden safety, then burn the safety through the "student syndrome" (start at the last minute) and Parkinson's Law (work expands to fill the time; early finishes are never reported) — so padding makes projects *later*, not safer ([PMI: critical chain project management](https://www.pmi.org/learning/library/critical-chain-project-management-5852); [Praxis Framework paper on critical chain](https://www.praxisframework.org/files/heptinstall-critical-chain.pdf)).
- Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002), "Procrastination, Deadlines, and Performance" (*Psychological Science* 13/3): people knowingly self-impose costly deadlines to fight procrastination, and those deadlines improve performance — but people set them suboptimally; externally imposed, evenly spaced deadlines beat self-set ones ([MIT PDF](https://web.mit.edu/ariely/www/MIT/Papers/deadlines.pdf); [journal record](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1467-9280.00441)).

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** The kit order is a textbook planning-fallacy trap: the club takes the "inside view" ("we just need to pick a design, it'll take a week") when the reference class — how long kit decisions took *last* season, and the season before — says six to ten weeks. Combine with student syndrome (nobody engages until pre-season is visibly close) and the fixed 25–30 day door-to-door tail, and late kits are not bad luck; they are the statistically expected outcome. Ariely's finding supplies the fix: external, binding intermediate deadlines ("design locked by June 1, sizes by June 15") outperform good intentions.

**Article angles:**

1. "Your kit will be late, and science can prove it": the Buehler thesis study rewritten for a club secretary — including the devastating 99%-certain figure.
2. "Reference class forecasting for Sunday league": don't ask 'how long will it take us?'; ask 'how long did it take every club like us?' — a Flyvbjerg method piece at grassroots scale.
3. "The student syndrome wears shin pads": why giving the team three months to submit sizes produces exactly the same last-week scramble as giving them three weeks.
4. "Deadlines you'd pay for": Ariely & Wertenbroch's evidence that people *want* to be forced — the case for a club calendar with penalties.

**Commercial bridge:** *Ready to Play Thinking* — the honest response to the planning fallacy isn't asking clubs to plan better; it's shortening the part of the timeline that planning can't fix by making the design decision a selection instead of a creation.

## 4. Queues are invisible: production is 6–17% of the pipeline

**Core insight:** The most rigorous study ever done of the apparel supply chain found garments spent a 66-week pipeline mostly *waiting* — actual production was as little as 6–17% of the total — and the same anatomy governs a kit order, where the queue is the group chat.

**Supporting evidence:**

- In the mid-1980s the US textile and apparel industry commissioned Kurt Salmon Associates to analyse its supply chain. KSA found the average apparel pipeline was **66 weeks long, of which only 6–17% was actual production time**; the rest was inventory sitting in warehouses and goods in transit — a finding that launched the "Quick Response" movement ([Springer, "The Evolution of Quick Response Programs"](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-04313-0_1); [Kincade 1995, *Clothing and Textiles Research Journal*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0887302X9501300405); Umbrex [profile of Kurt Salmon](https://umbrex.com/resources/profiles-of-the-top-consulting-firms/overview-profile-and-history-of-kurt-salmon-acquired-by-accenture/)).
- Zara proved the pipeline was compressible: design-to-shelf in roughly two to three weeks versus six months or more for traditional retail, via vertical integration and small fast batches; ~12 inventory turns per year against competitors' 3–4 ([Supply Chain 24/7 on Zara](https://www.supplychain247.com/article/zaras_fashion_retail_supply_chain_strategies); [SCM Globe case study](https://www.scmglobe.com/zara-clothing-company-supply-chain/)).
- Donald Reinertsen, *The Principles of Product Development Flow* (2009): queues, not work, are the primary driver of slow product development — and "product development queues are invisible" because they're not piles of physical inventory but ideas waiting for decisions. His first commandment: "If you only quantify one thing, quantify the Cost of Delay." He reports ~85% of product managers don't know their cost of delay, and intuitive estimates of it vary by 50-to-1 ([book notes](https://joecotellese.com/posts/principles-of-product-development-flow-book-summary/); [Cost of delay, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost_of_delay); [SE-Trends summary of the 175 flow principles](https://www.se-trends.de/en/the-175-flow-principles-why-product-development-is-often-slower-than-necessary/)).

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** Run the KSA arithmetic on a kit order. If a club spends eight weeks deciding and collecting sizes before a 25–30 day door-to-door fulfilment, production (7–12 days) is roughly 10–15% of the total elapsed time — almost exactly KSA's 6–17% figure, forty years later, at miniature scale. The queue is invisible for exactly Reinertsen's reason: an unmade decision doesn't sit on a shelf where anyone can see it. And the cost of delay is real but never quantified: training in mismatched shirts, a sponsor's logo missing from the season's first photos, players buying interim gear that duplicates spend.

**Article angles:**

1. "The 66-week shirt": the forgotten 1980s study that measured how little of a garment's life is spent being made — and what the same stopwatch says about a grassroots kit.
2. "The queue you can't see": Reinertsen's invisible-queue principle applied to the unplaced order sitting in a chat thread — with a worked 'cost of delay' calculation for one club season.
3. "What Zara knew": quick response as a philosophy — the radical idea that the pipeline, not the factory, is the product.

**Commercial bridge:** *Ready to Play Thinking* — Ready to Play is quick-response logic applied to design: finished, customizable designs are the equivalent of Zara's pre-positioned fabric, moving the club's start line past the longest queue.

## 5. Approval latency: what construction's RFI data reveals about sign-off

**Core insight:** The best-measured approval workflow in any industry — the construction RFI — shows that a single request for a decision takes about ten days to answer, costs about $1,080 to process, and nearly a quarter of the time is never answered at all; kit sign-offs run on the same physics without the measurement.

**Supporting evidence:**

- The Navigant Construction Forum's 2013 study of 1,362 projects containing over one million RFIs (requests for information — the construction industry's formal 'we need a decision before we can proceed' instrument): median reply time ~9.7 days (average first reply 6.4 days, up to 10 days by region/size); ~9.9 RFIs per $1 million of construction (~800 per average project); ~8 hours and ~$1,080 of review cost per RFI; cumulative project cost approaching $859,000 — and roughly **22–23% of RFIs never receive any response** ([CMAA/Navigant: "Impact & Control of RFIs on Construction Projects" PDF](https://www.cmaanet.org/sites/default/files/resource/Impact%20&%20Control%20of%20RFIs%20on%20Construction%20Projects.pdf); [Procore's RFI guide](https://www.procore.com/library/rfi-construction); [Helonic, RFI response-time benchmarks](https://helonic.com/blog/construction-rfi-response-time-benchmarks)).
- A documented grassroots-adjacent case: Real Bedford FC (the Bitcoin-backed non-league club) published a club statement in 2023 detailing kit delays from supplier Macron — designs approved July 13 and paid in full (>£50,000 across men's, ladies' and 21 girls' youth teams), followed by a 5–6 week order-processing delay for senior teams, and the youth order still unprocessed nine weeks after payment ([Real Bedford FC club statement](https://www.realbedford.com/news/club-statement-regarding-delays-from-macron-in-delivery-of-our-23/24-season-playing-kits)). Note the timeline: even a well-funded, professionally run club approving designs in mid-July left zero buffer against a supply-side queue.
- The pattern generalises: each round-trip in an approval chain ("can the sponsor confirm the logo placement?", "does the committee approve the away colours?") is an RFI in all but name — an information request that blocks downstream work while it waits, with a latency measured in days-to-weeks and a real probability of simply dying unanswered.

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** A kit order typically embeds three to five informal RFIs: design approval, sponsor logo file and placement approval, size confirmation from every player, budget sign-off, final proof approval. At construction-grade latency (call it a week each, with a one-in-five chance any given one stalls indefinitely), the approval chain alone consumes a month before production starts — and unlike construction, nobody at a club logs, tracks or escalates these requests. The 23% never-answered figure is the statistical shape of "waiting on two lads for their sizes since March."

**Article angles:**

1. "The £1,080 question": what construction learned by actually measuring approval delays — and the informal RFIs hiding inside every kit order.
2. "Approved July 13, still waiting in September": the Real Bedford case as a forensic timeline of where kit weeks actually go (including the supply side's own queues).
3. "The unanswered 23%": on requests that die — why every approval chain needs an owner, a deadline and an escalation path, even at a Sunday league club.

**Commercial bridge:** *Studio Notes* — document MOMUTO's own approval touchpoints honestly (proof approval, logo file checks) and how each is designed to be a single round-trip; the credibility comes from admitting approval latency exists on both sides of the counter.

## 6. The volunteer kit manager is context-switching to death

**Core insight:** The person running a grassroots kit order is doing it in the residue between a day job, family and training — and the research on task switching says that mode of working doesn't just slow the task, it multiplies elapsed time by an order of magnitude.

**Supporting evidence:**

- Gerald Weinberg, *Quality Software Management: Systems Thinking* (1992): his much-cited estimate is that a second concurrent project costs ~20% of productive time to switching overhead, a third leaves ~20% per project, and five concurrent projects waste up to ~75% of capacity. These are engineering heuristics, not lab results — Weinberg says so — but nothing more rigorous has displaced them ([Scrum.org, "The Financial Cost of Task Switching"](https://www.scrum.org/resources/blog/financial-cost-task-switching); [Coding Horror, "The Multi-Tasking Myth"](https://blog.codinghorror.com/the-multi-tasking-myth/)).
- Gloria Mark (UC Irvine): observational studies of knowledge workers found that after an interruption it takes on average about 23 minutes and 15 seconds to return to the original task at the same level of focus, with workers interrupted (or self-interrupting) every few minutes ([summary with citations](https://addyo.substack.com/p/it-takes-23-mins-to-recover-after); [Nimea overview of the research](https://nimea.app/en/blog/focus-and-interruptions)).
- Sophie Leroy (2009), "Why is it so hard to do my work?" (*Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*): coined **attention residue** — when switching from task A to task B, part of attention remains on A, degrading performance on B; residue shrinks dramatically when a task is explicitly closed out before switching ([research overview](https://get-alfred.ai/blog/attention-residue); Rubinstein, Meyer & Evans 2001 provide the underlying lab evidence on switching costs).

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** A kit order handled by a volunteer in ten-minute evening fragments — chase two sizes, wait, forward a logo, wait, re-open the design debate — is a project run entirely inside switching overhead. Each fragment pays the 23-minute re-entry tax on a 10-minute task, and Leroy's work explains why the order feels mentally heavy out of all proportion to its size: it's never closed out, so it leaks attention for weeks. This is a structural argument for reducing the number of open loops in the ordering process (one decision, one collection pass, one approval), not for finding a more heroic volunteer.

**Article angles:**

1. "The kit order that lived in your head for three months": attention residue and the psychic weight of an unfinished order — why volunteers burn out on admin, not on football.
2. "Nine minutes a night": a diary-style reconstruction of how a simple order becomes a twelve-week project when it's done in fragments, with the Weinberg/Mark numbers as the spine.
3. "Close the loop": the single most evidence-backed piece of advice for club admins — batch the order into two or three sittings with explicit closure, instead of thirty micro-sessions.

**Commercial bridge:** *Football Reality* — the volunteer's evening, told truthfully, is the strongest editorial territory MOMUTO has: the audience is the person doing this at 9:40pm, and the research dignifies their exhaustion as a measurable phenomenon rather than a personal failing.
