# The Creative Process: How Great Football Kits Are Actually Conceived

**Editorial research dossier — MOMUTO "Studio Notes" pillar**
Researched August 2026. All claims sourced; URLs recorded at point of use and in the Sources list.

## Abstract

The public story of a football kit is a launch video and a marketing line. The real story is a two-to-three-year industrial design process governed by an unusually dense stack of constraints: federation politics, FIFA and league regulations, broadcast physics, sponsor real estate, player physiology, and manufacturing reality. This dossier documents how professional kit designers at Nike, adidas, and Umbro actually work — their timelines, research methods, and iteration loops — and connects that practice to three bodies of evidence that grassroots clubs rarely see: the optics of why patterns behave differently on camera than in person, the psychology literature showing that constraints reliably *improve* creative output (the "Green Eggs and Ham hypothesis"), and the long tradition of importing ideas from architecture, tailoring, and graphic design into sportswear. The through-line for MOMUTO: the things amateur clubs assume disqualify them from "real" design — templates, tight budgets, rules, small canvases — are precisely the conditions under which professional kit design already operates.

## Topic 1 — The pipeline: kit design is a two-to-three-year research project, not a sketch

**Core insight:** A professional football kit begins two to three years before kickoff and is shaped less by a single designer's vision than by structured research — fan listening sessions, player workshops, federation negotiation, and multiple formal feedback rounds.

**Supporting evidence:**

- adidas's Director of Federations, Mateo Kossmann, describes the federation jersey as "a long, collaborative journey that takes between 18 and 24 months," a shared process with milestones rather than either party dictating terms; work on the 2026 World Cup jerseys began **three years** before launch. adidas builds each kit "through multiple rounds of feedback," combining fan insight research, the federation's perspective, and the seasonal identity of adidas Football. (FashionUnited, "26 Nations, 26 Stories," 2026 — https://fashionunited.in/news/fashion/26-nations-26-stories-how-adidas-navigates-global-culture-through-football-kit-design/2026042254116 ; ESPN, "What makes a good World Cup jersey?", 2026 — https://www.espn.com/soccer/story/_/id/49027527/world-cup-2026-makes-good-jersey-democratic-concepts-authentic-storytelling-nostalgia)
- Nike's 2026 US national team kits launched March 2026 but the process began in **spring 2023**. Nike calls its method "DIG" (Discover, Ideate, Go): designers travelled to cities across the country interviewing fans before any design work, then ran design workshops and listening sessions where USMNT players gave input on fabric weight, seam placement, fit, mobility, and breathability. (U.S. Soccer, "Behind the Stars & Stripes," 2026 — https://www.ussoccer.com/stories/2026/03/stars-stripes-nike-process-details-national-team-world-cup-jersey-kit)
- Nike's stated production arc runs from 2D design to 3D development to prototyping to rigorous testing, combining design, scientific research, brand strategy, marketing and retail; the brand times new material platforms ("a chassis for the kit based on a new fabric or yarn") to tournament cycles like Euros and World Cups. (Footy Headlines, "How A Nike Elite Team Kit Is Made," 2021 — https://www.footyheadlines.com/2021/01/how-nike-elite-team-kit-is-made-from.html)
- Player language drives aesthetic decisions: when Nike asked players what they wanted to *feel* in a jersey, answers like "sophisticated, luxurious, swag" translated directly into a richer colour palette. (U.S. Soccer / Fast Company coverage of the 2026 kits — https://www.fastcompany.com/91510417/us-soccer-world-cup-jerseys-2026 ; https://www.fastcompany.com/91565654/how-nike-adidas-designed-world-cup-uniforms-balancing-national-pride-high-tech-comfort)
- adidas pairs the storytelling layer with a performance layer developed in parallel — e.g. Climacool+ with body-mapped 3D structure varying breathability by body zone in the 2026 federation kits. (CBS Sports, 2025 — https://www.cbssports.com/soccer/news/fifa-world-cup-2026-jerseys-adidas-kits-22-nations-summers-tournament ; adidas Group, "A Jersey Reinvented," 2025 — https://www.adidas-group.com/en/magazine/behind-the-scenes/a-jersey-reinvented-the-adidas-2025-federation-kits)

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** The gap between a Sunday league kit and a World Cup kit is not talent — it is process. Almost none of the big-brand pipeline (fan listening, player wear-testing, structured feedback rounds) requires a big-brand budget; it requires asking your own players and supporters the same questions Nike asks, earlier. A grassroots club that runs even one structured "what should this shirt feel like?" session with its squad is doing a miniature version of the DIG phase.

**Article angles:**

1. "Three years for ninety minutes" — a timeline anatomy of a single World Cup shirt, from first fan interview to kickoff, contrasted with the three weeks a grassroots club typically gives itself.
2. "Nike asks players how a shirt should *feel*. When did anyone ask your Sunday league captain?" — translating pro research methods into a one-page toolkit for club kit decisions.
3. "The kit is a committee — and that's fine" — why the myth of the lone designer misdescribes even elite kit design, and what healthy multi-stakeholder feedback rounds look like at any level.

**Commercial bridge:** Studio Notes (how MOMUTO compresses the pro pipeline for clubs) with a secondary fit for Ready to Play Thinking (structured briefs beat blank pages).

## Topic 2 — Permission and precedent: what the Nigeria 2018 shirt reveals about creative latitude

**Core insight:** The most celebrated kit of the modern era was not a stroke of inspiration but an act of *permission* — a designer abstracting a 24-year-old archive reference, backed by a federation and leadership explicitly willing to "push it as far as we could."

**Supporting evidence:**

- Designer Matthew Wolff (then at Nike, previously in-house designer at NYCFC where he created the club badge) built the Nigeria 2018 home shirt from the 1994 World Cup jersey's black-and-white eagle-wing sleeves — abstracting the wing into a "feathery chevron pattern" running down the front. (AIGA Eye on Design, "How Nike Designed Nigeria's Amazingly Graphical World Cup Strip," 2018 — https://eyeondesign.aiga.org/how-nike-designed-nigerias-amazingly-graphical-world-cup-strip/ ; Matthew Wolff, Wikipedia — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_Wolff_(designer))
- Wolff on the enabling conditions: "a lot of credit goes to the Nigerian Federation and the Nike leadership, who encouraged us to really push it as far as we could." Nigeria had previously worn plain, off-the-shelf stock jerseys — the bespoke treatment itself was the surprise. (AIGA Eye on Design, 2018, as above)
- The result: the shirt sold out in roughly three hours at its London launch and became a global fashion phenomenon far beyond football. (The FADER, "How Nike turned Nigeria's World Cup kit into a fashion phenomenon," 2018 — https://www.thefader.com/2018/06/11/nigeria-world-cup-team-jersey ; Skidmore College profile of Wolff, 2018 — https://www.skidmore.edu/news/2018/0618-matt-wolff.php)
- The archive-abstraction method generalises: Craig Buglass, the Nike designer behind the 2002 World Cup kits (Brazil, Portugal, South Korea, USA, Nigeria and others), describes a philosophy of using kit cycles to project forward — "You can create the future" — rather than merely decorating fabric. (SportsLogos.net, "Designer of 2002 Nike World Cup Kits Looks Back," 2022 — https://news.sportslogos.net/2022/09/09/you-can-create-the-future-designer-of-2002-nike-world-cup-kits-looks-back-on-what-inspired-them/soccer/ ; The Football Kit Podcast, Craig Buglass episode, 2021 — https://thefootballkitpodcast.com/2021/06/09/episode-five-craig-buglass-has-just-done-it/)
- Wolff's independent work (Oakland Roots, USL clubs) is explicitly framed as channelling *civic pride* into design — the identity comes from the place, not the designer. (PRINT Magazine designer interview — https://www.printmag.com/designer-interviews/matthew-wolff/ ; Oakland Roots Q+A — https://medium.com/@oaklandroots/q-a-with-matthew-wolff-a9ee24ea6b50)

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** Every club — even one founded in 2019 — has an archive: an old badge, a founding story, a colour someone's grandfather chose. Nigeria 2018 shows the method is (a) find the authentic reference, (b) abstract it rather than reproduce it, (c) get the committee to grant permission to be bold. The scarcest resource in kit design is not budget or skill; it is a chairperson willing to say yes to something distinctive.

**Article angles:**

1. "The yes before the design" — how the boldest kits in history were enabled by permission structures, and how a grassroots kit committee can deliberately grant itself the same licence.
2. "Abstraction, not nostalgia" — the 1994→2018 Nigeria method as a repeatable technique: take your club's oldest visual asset and push it three steps away from literal.
3. "Every club has a 1994" — a practical archaeology guide: where amateur clubs can dig for their own archive references (badges, town heraldry, old team photos, ground architecture).
4. "From stock shirt to statement" — Nigeria went from off-the-shelf jerseys to a design icon in one cycle; what that leap says about the difference identity investment makes.

**Commercial bridge:** Design Culture (the story itself), with Studio Notes as the how-to companion (MOMUTO's own archive-abstraction exercises with clubs).

## Topic 3 — Pattern mechanics: rhythm, scale, contrast — and why the camera is a design constraint

**Core insight:** A kit pattern is engineered for at least three viewing distances at once — touchline, terrace, and television — and the television imposes hard optical limits (moiré, aliasing, contrast) that quietly govern what professionals will and won't put on a shirt.

**Supporting evidence:**

- Fine, high-contrast repeating stripes shimmer and "crawl" on camera: the moiré effect arises when the fabric's pattern frequency interferes with the camera sensor's sampling grid and the display's pixel grid, producing low-frequency "beat" bands that appear to move. Three effects combine — moiré interference, sampling aliasing, and temporal beating with frame rate — which is why broadcast's oldest wardrobe rule is "never wear fine stripes." (SAS/The DO Loop, "Moire patterns: why you shouldn't wear a striped shirt on video," 2023 — https://blogs.sas.com/content/iml/2023/06/05/moire-patterns.html ; PLSN, "A Word About Moiré" — https://plsn.com/articles/video-world/a-word-about-moire/ ; ScienceInsights, "What Is Moiré?" — https://scienceinsights.org/what-is-moire-how-the-interference-effect-forms/)
- Sponsors need quiet zones: teamwear logo-placement guidance is explicit that on striped, gradient, or intricately patterned shirts, logos must sit on more *uniform* background areas to avoid blending into busy visuals, and that high figure-ground contrast is the primary legibility variable. Standard architecture: club crest left chest, manufacturer right chest, sponsor centre-front below the collar. (Miles Teamwear, "Logo Placement Guide" — https://miles-teamwear.com/blogs/news/logo-placement-guide-for-teamwear ; Wooter, "Logo Placement Rules for Soccer Jerseys" — https://wooter.com/articles/logo-placement-rules-soccer-jerseys/)
- Numbers and names are regulated design objects: FIFA mandates back numbers of roughly 10–14 inches and defined sleeve free zones (~12cm × 8cm); pattern choice (stripes, hoops, gradients) is expected to *support* number readability, not fight it. (Luson Sport, "What Is a Football Kit?" — https://luson.com/what-is-a-football-kit/ ; The Live Football App, "How football kits balance design and sponsorship" — https://www.thelivefootballapp.com/football-kits-sponsorship/)
- Legibility is now empirically tested at league level: for the Premier League's 2023–24 redesign of names and numbers, commentary experts were stationed both live in stadiums and watching match footage to score colour-contrast combinations, and clubs must now choose lettering from pre-approved style/colour combinations — Manchester City's custom Noel Gallagher-handwriting font was permissible in the Champions League but not the Premier League. (ESPN, "Premier League kit names, numbers and logo redesigned for 2023-24," 2023 — https://www.espn.com/soccer/story/_/id/37637226/premier-league-kit-names-numbers-logo-redesigned-2023-24-season ; ESPN on Man City's font, 2024 — https://www.espn.co.uk/football/story/_/id/40392536/manchester-city-names-numbers-kits-font-champions-league-noel-gallagher ; nss sports on the universal font — https://www.nss-sports.com/en/lifestyle/32701/font-premier-league)
- The general distance rule professionals apply: shirts must be recognisable at a glance from far away, so bold colour-blocking and clear pattern rhythm beat intricate detail that only reads in hand. (The Live Football App, as above)

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** Grassroots kits are increasingly *filmed* — Veo cameras, phone highlights, livestreamed cup finals — which means the broadcast constraints now apply at step-over level, and most amateur kit buyers have never heard of them. Pattern scale, number contrast, and sponsor quiet zones are the difference between a shirt that looks sharp in the club-shop render and one that turns to visual noise in match footage. This is teachable, checkable craft.

**Article angles:**

1. "Why your kit strobes on the highlights reel" — the physics of moiré explained for club secretaries, with a simple pattern-scale rule of thumb before ordering.
2. "The three-distance test" — evaluate any kit design at arm's length, from the far touchline, and through a phone camera; how pros design for all three simultaneously.
3. "Quiet zones: the invisible architecture of a shirt" — mapping the reserved real estate (crest, maker, sponsor, number) and how great designs treat those blanks as part of the composition, not interruptions to it.
4. "The Premier League hired commentators to test fonts" — legibility as a first-class design discipline, and what a grassroots league could learn from it.

**Commercial bridge:** Ready to Play Thinking (practical pre-order checks), with Studio Notes as the venue for MOMUTO's own pattern-scale testing practice.

## Topic 4 — The constraint paradox: the science says limits make design better

**Core insight:** Peer-reviewed creativity research consistently finds that well-chosen constraints produce *more* creative output than total freedom — meaning the grassroots kit brief, with its fixed colours, budget, and rules, is closer to an ideal creative environment than a blank canvas would be.

**Supporting evidence:**

- The "Green Eggs and Ham hypothesis" (Haught-Tromp, *Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts*, 2017): in two experiments, participants writing two-line rhymes produced measurably more creative work when forced to include an externally imposed noun; the effect even carried over, with mere practice under constraints stimulating later creativity. The name honours Dr. Seuss, whose publisher challenged him to write a book using no more than 50 distinct words — the result was *Green Eggs and Ham*, one of the best-selling children's books ever. (APA record — https://psycnet.apa.org/psycinfo/2016-17972-001/ ; full paper PDF — https://www.cct.umb.edu/630/files/HaughtTromp2017-GreenEggsandHam.pdf ; Pacific Standard summary — https://psmag.com/news/constraints-can-be-a-catalyst-for-creativity/)
- Rosso (*Organization Studies*, 2014) studied R&D teams and found constraints helped creativity specifically when teams "saw opportunity in constraints" — the framing, not just the constraint, determines the benefit. (https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0170840613517600)
- The broader literature describes an inverted-U: too few constraints produce complacency and cliché, too many suffocate; "the right amount" of constraint focuses the search space and pushes creators off their default solutions. Deliberate restriction is repeatedly found to be a greater enhancer of creativity than total freedom. (Tromp & Sternberg et al., *Creativity from constraints: Theory and applications to education*, Thinking Skills & Creativity, 2022 — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1871187122001870 ; Brill, *Constraints in Creativity* (22-researcher volume) — https://brill.com/display/title/70810?language=en ; Open College of the Arts — https://www.oca.ac.uk/weareoca/illustration/can-constraints-improve-our-creativity/)
- Kit design is a live case study of the inverted-U in industry: designers operate inside FIFA/league regulations, federation approval gates, fixed crest/sponsor/number zones, and manufacturing limits — yet the field's most acclaimed work (Nigeria 2018, the 2002 Nike World Cup generation) came from exactly these conditions, with designers crediting the framework rather than resenting it (see Topics 1–3).

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** Grassroots clubs often experience their limits — two club colours, a league's clash rules, one sponsor who must be kept happy, a template-based product — as evidence they can't have "real" design. The research says the opposite: those constraints are the raw material of a strong brief. The productive move is Rosso's reframe — treat each limit as a design prompt ("the sponsor block is fixed → what pattern makes that block look intentional?") rather than a deduction from ambition.

**Article angles:**

1. "Green eggs and grass stains" — the 50-word-book experiment retold for kit design: what a two-colour, one-sponsor, league-legal brief makes *possible*.
2. "The blank canvas is a trap" — why clubs that start from 'anything goes' end up with clichés, and how writing a tighter brief produces a more original shirt.
3. "Design like an R&D team" — Rosso's finding that constraint-framing predicts creative success, turned into a kit-committee exercise.
4. "The inverted U of kit briefs" — diagnosing whether your club's brief is under-constrained (vague, trend-chasing) or over-constrained (design by veto), and how to find the productive middle.

**Commercial bridge:** Ready to Play Thinking (constraint-first briefing is the core of the product experience), secondary Studio Notes (MOMUTO's brief templates as applied constraint theory).

## Topic 5 — Inspiration transfer: architecture, tailoring, and graphic art keep re-entering football through the shirt

**Core insight:** The most memorable kits are rarely "sports design" at all — they are translations from adjacent disciplines (Savile Row tailoring, album-cover graphic design, temple architecture, ceramic tiles), and the translation step, not the source, is the craft.

**Supporting evidence:**

- **Tailoring → performance wear:** Umbro's 2009 England home kit was created by a team pairing senior designer David Blanch with RCA-trained designer Aitor Throup and Savile Row-trained tailor Charlie Allen. They "deconstructed the traditional kit and evaluated every stitch and seam in relation to its purpose in football": darted shoulders, ribbed vented underarms, flattened inside seams, a single-button placket, shorts in multiple length fittings, even repositioned sock seams — bespoke tailoring logic applied to a football shirt, branded "Tailored by Umbro." (Designboom, "'Tailored by Umbro' / Aitor Throup interview" — https://www.designboom.com/design/tailored-by-umbro-aitor-throup-interview/ ; Football Shirt Culture, 2009 — https://www.footballshirtculture.com/0910-kits/new-england-kit-2009-launched.html ; FashionUnited on the 2025 Throup reunion — https://fashionunited.uk/news/fashion/umbro-reuniting-with-designer-aitor-throup/2025061982328)
- **Graphic design → national identity:** Peter Saville (Joy Division's *Unknown Pleasures* sleeve) designed the 2010 England kit's shoulder print for Umbro: small red, blue, green and purple St George's crosses, deliberately evoking tailors' basting stitches, intended to "reclaim" the cross and represent a nation "now one of the most culturally diverse in the world" (Saville). The motif later evolved into interlocking-cross away designs. (Dezeen, "England Home Kit by Peter Saville for Umbro," 2010 — https://www.dezeen.com/2010/09/02/england-home-kit-by-peter-saville-for-umbro/ ; BBC News, 2010 — https://feeds.bbci.co.uk/news/entertainment-arts-11176405 ; Hypebeast, 2011 — https://hypebeast.com/2011/2/umbro-modern-england-collection-by-peter-saville)
- **Architecture → pattern:** Cambodia's national kits carry a woven repeating pattern derived from the *aerial view* of Angkor Wat's five central towers; Glasgow's Braves wear a fractal pattern fusing tartan with the work of architect-designer Charles Rennie Mackintosh; CD Castellón's away shirt lifts a linear pattern from local ceramic tile art. (ESPN, "More of 2024-25's most stylish kits you probably missed," 2024 — https://www.espn.com/soccer/story/_/id/41971293/more-2024-2025-most-stylish-kits-probably-missed)
- **Design culture claims the whole game:** It's Nice That's survey of football graphic design — badges, fanzines, typography, kits — argues "wherever you look in the world of football, there's a design behind it," treating the game as one of the most pervasive vernacular design cultures in existence. (It's Nice That, "Designing the beautiful game," 2022 — https://www.itsnicethat.com/features/football-designing-the-beautiful-game-graphic-design-090522)
- The mechanism repeats: in every case the source (a temple, a stitch, a tile) is abstracted into a *repeating system* that survives sublimation printing, viewing distance, and the shirt's fixed zones — the same abstraction discipline as Nigeria 2018 (Topic 2).

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** A club's town is full of unexploited source material — Victorian brickwork, a canal bridge, market-hall tiles, a local tartan or textile trade. The pro method is not "hire Peter Saville"; it is: choose one authentic local source, abstract it into a repeatable pattern, and test it at distance. That method scales down to a one-club order far more readily than any trend does, and it produces shirts nobody else in the league could have worn.

**Article angles:**

1. "Steal from buildings, not from Nike" — a field guide to sourcing kit patterns from local architecture, with the Angkor Wat and Mackintosh cases as method demonstrations.
2. "The tailor who took apart the England shirt" — the 2009 Umbro story as an argument that construction (fit, seams, drape) is design, not just decoration; what 'considered construction' means at teamwear price points.
3. "Basting stitches on the nation's shoulders" — Saville's multicoloured crosses as a masterclass in loading meaning into a tiny, quiet detail — and why small clubs should think detail-first, not billboard-first.
4. "Football is Britain's biggest design museum" — riffing on It's Nice That: the case that kit culture is vernacular design history, and grassroots clubs are its living archive.

**Commercial bridge:** Design Culture (the stories), feeding Studio Notes (MOMUTO's local-source pattern development process as a documented practice).

## Topic 6 — Design systems and templates: the honest case for structured creativity

**Core insight:** The industry's real divide is not "template versus bespoke" but whether a design system is used as a dead catalogue or as a living framework — leagues, brands, and the best studios all deploy systems (approved fonts, adaptable foundations, modular platforms) precisely to make good outcomes repeatable.

**Supporting evidence:**

- The Premier League's universal name-and-number system (2023–24) is a league-scale design system: one redesigned letterform family and a fixed palette of pre-approved style/colour combinations, empirically contrast-tested, applied across all twenty clubs — trading individual club expression for guaranteed legibility and a coherent league identity. (ESPN, 2023 — https://www.espn.com/soccer/story/_/id/37637226/premier-league-kit-names-numbers-logo-redesigned-2023-24-season ; nss sports — https://www.nss-sports.com/en/lifestyle/32701/font-premier-league)
- The teamwear market openly operates on a spectrum: adidas miTeam lets clubs build from a base of current adidas teamwear designs; SWAZ markets "hundreds of templates" alongside fully bespoke routes; specialist providers position themselves against "generic kit templates designed to promote global brands" by starting from each club's badge, colours and history. The template is the industry's load-bearing structure — the differentiation is in how much authorship sits on top of it. (Kitlocker on adidas miTeam — https://kitlocker.com/blog/adidas-miteam/ ; SWAZ — https://swaz.co.uk/ ; CORE Teamwear — https://www.coreteamwear.co.uk/)
- Even elite bespoke work is platform-based: Nike's tournament kits are built on shared material "chassis" innovations rolled out across many federations at once (Topic 1), and adidas designs 22+ national kits per World Cup as one coherent seasonal system with per-nation cultural layers. (Footy Headlines, 2021 — https://www.footyheadlines.com/2021/01/how-nike-elite-team-kit-is-made-from.html ; FashionUnited, 2026 — https://fashionunited.in/news/fashion/26-nations-26-stories-how-adidas-navigates-global-culture-through-football-kit-design/2026042254116)
- The constraint literature (Topic 4) predicts exactly this: a good template functions as scaffolding that focuses the search space — the inverted-U's productive middle — while a bad template experience is simply an over-constrained brief with zero authorship left in it. (Tromp et al., 2022 — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1871187122001870)

**Why this matters for grassroots football:** Grassroots clubs feel template shame — the fear of turning up in the same shirt as three other teams in the league. The useful reframe: professionals never start from zero either; they start from systems and spend their creativity on the layer that carries identity (pattern, colour logic, detail, story). A club choosing where to invest its limited authorship — one signature pattern, one meaningful detail, one disciplined colour system — is behaving exactly like a pro studio, not settling.

**Article angles:**

1. "Nobody designs from zero" — from Premier League fonts to World Cup fabric chassis: the hidden systems under every 'bespoke' kit, and why systems are a feature, not a compromise.
2. "Template shame is a category error" — the difference between a catalogue kit and a system kit is authorship, not price; a framework for deciding where your club's one distinctive move should go.
3. "A design system for a 60-member club" — what the minimum viable club identity system looks like (colours, crest rules, number style, one pattern), inspired by how leagues codify theirs.

**Commercial bridge:** Studio Notes (MOMUTO's own system-plus-authorship model is the working proof), secondary Football Reality (what clubs can actually afford and why systems make good design accessible).
