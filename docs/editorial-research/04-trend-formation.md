# Trend Formation: The Machinery Behind How Design Trends Emerge, Spread and Die

**Research dossier — MOMUTO editorial. Area 04. Compiled August 2026.**

**Abstract.** Trends are not weather; they are machinery. A century of research across rural sociology, fashion history, network science and consumer psychology shows that design trends follow describable mechanics: they diffuse along an S-curve through identifiable adopter types (Rogers, building on the 1943 Iowa hybrid-corn studies); they are increasingly *manufactured* rather than predicted by a paid forecasting industry (WGSN and peers) whose forecasts function as self-fulfilling prophecies; they recycle on a roughly 20–30-year nostalgia pendulum first formalised by fashion historian James Laver in 1937; they flow in two directions — trickling down from elites (Simmel, 1904) and bubbling up from subcultures (Blumberg, Polhemus, Hebdige) — with football's terrace culture being one of history's clearest bubble-up cases; and they die by predictable rules, the most striking being Berger & Le Mens's finding that the faster a cultural taste is adopted, the faster it is abandoned. Football kit design sits at the intersection of every one of these mechanisms, and grassroots clubs are the "late majority" that most trend literature describes but never talks to. This dossier maps the machinery so MOMUTO can document it — and explain how ideas travel from a Milan runway or a TikTok feed to a Sunday-league pitch.

## 1. The S-Curve: Every Trend Is Corn — Diffusion of Innovations

**Core insight.** New designs spread through populations the way hybrid corn spread through Iowa farms in the 1930s: not on merit alone, but through a social process with five distinct adopter types, an S-shaped curve, and a dangerous gap in the middle where most innovations die.

**Supporting evidence.**
- The founding study of all trend science is agricultural: rural sociologists Bryce Ryan and Neal Gross interviewed 259 Iowa farmers in 1943 about why they were slow to adopt hybrid seed corn despite its clear superiority, discovering that adoption was a social process driven by peer influence and communication channels, not rational evaluation ([ERIC review of Rogers' theory, Sahin 2006](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED501453.pdf); [TheoryHub, Newcastle University](https://open.ncl.ac.uk/theories/8/diffusion-of-innovations/)).
- Everett Rogers synthesised 508 diffusion studies into *Diffusion of Innovations* (1962, five editions to 2003), formalising the adopter categories — Innovators (2.5%), Early Adopters (13.5%), Early Majority (34%), Late Majority (34%), Laggards (16%) — and the cumulative S-curve ([TheoryHub](https://open.ncl.ac.uk/theories/8/diffusion-of-innovations/); [AgriFutures Extension Practice](https://extension-practice-agrifutures.com.au/resource/diffusion-of-innovations-theory-adoption-and-diffusion/)).
- Rogers identified five attributes of the innovation itself that determine whether the curve takes off at all: relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, and observability ([TheoryHub](https://open.ncl.ac.uk/theories/8/diffusion-of-innovations/)). Football shirts score maximally on *observability* — a kit is worn in public, photographed, broadcast — which is precisely why kit design trends diffuse faster than most product categories.
- Geoffrey Moore's *Crossing the Chasm* (1991) later argued that in technology markets there is a structural gap between the early adopters (who buy on vision) and the early majority (who buy on reference from people like themselves) — the point where most innovations stall ([overview via Yu-kai Chou](https://yukaichou.com/gamification-analysis/diffusion-of-innovations-rogers-adoption-curve/)).

**Why this matters for grassroots football.** Amateur clubs are, almost by definition, Rogers's early/late majority: they adopt a design language only after it has been de-risked by professional clubs, big brands and other local teams ("compatibility" and "observability" in Rogers's terms). A grassroots kit decision — usually made by a committee, on a budget, once every two or three seasons — is a textbook late-majority adoption event. Understanding where a given aesthetic sits on the S-curve tells an amateur club whether they're buying a look with five years of life left or five months.

**Article angles.**
1. "Every trend is corn": how a 1943 study of Iowa farmers explains why your Sunday-league club just ordered a 90s-style collar — the founding experiment of trend science, retold through kit design.
2. "The kit chasm": pro clubs are the innovators, fashion-forward fan brands the early adopters — so who carries a kit trend across the chasm to the amateur game, and why do most never make it?
3. The observability advantage: why football shirts diffuse trends faster than almost any other designed object (they are worn, broadcast, and photographed by definition).
4. Mapping one real trend (e.g. the return of the polo collar, or gradient sublimation) onto Rogers's five attributes to show exactly why it spread or stalled.

**Commercial bridge.** *Ready to Play Thinking* — helps club decision-makers understand where on the adoption curve their kit choice sits, reframing MOMUTO's role as the bridge between early-adopter design culture and majority-market club reality.

## 2. The Trend Industrial Complex: How Forecasting Became Manufacturing

**Core insight.** The world's dominant trend forecaster doesn't so much predict fashion as coordinate it: when nearly every major brand subscribes to the same forecast, the forecast becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy — and the "spontaneous" trends that reach the high street were, in large part, scheduled years in advance.

**Supporting evidence.**
- WGSN — originally Worth Global Style Network, founded in London in 1997–98 by Marc and Julian Worth — serves thousands of clients in nearly 100 countries, from Nike and H&M to Starbucks, NBC and Nickelodeon, behind a paywall costing tens of thousands of dollars per subscription ([99% Invisible, "The Trend Forecast," ep. 229](https://99percentinvisible.org/episode/the-trend-forecast/); [WGSN](https://www.wgsn.com/en/wgsn)).
- WGSN forecasts up to five seasons (~18 months+) ahead, organised around the product-to-market lifecycle, and now claims "94% accuracy up to a year ahead" via its TrendCurve AI model, which combines social, search, retail-shelf, catwalk and consumer-sentiment data reviewed by in-house analysts on every continent ([WGSN Fashion Buying](https://www.wgsn.com/en/wgsn-fashion-buying); [WGSN product pages](https://www.wgsn.com/en/products/fashion-design)).
- The central critique, made openly within the industry: "WGSN is not predicting trends, it is making them… their slogan is 'creating tomorrow'" — because buyers and designers across almost all high-street brands base collections on the same forecasts, the projections become reality, at the cost of homogenisation ([Luiza do Prado Lima, Medium](https://luizadopradolima.medium.com/do-you-believe-in-oracles-trend-forecasting-is-trending-in-the-fashion-industry-a6cb8ed2acf4); [Fashion Theory Co., "WGSN & Fashion Forecasting"](https://fashiontheoryco.substack.com/p/wgsn-and-fashion-forecasting); [Meer, "How fashion trends are born"](https://www.meer.com/en/100542-how-fashion-trends-are-born-and-why-they-matter)).
- The profession's street-level prehistory: Malcolm Gladwell's "The Coolhunt" (*The New Yorker*, 17 March 1997) coined "coolhunting" while following DeeDee Gordon and Baysie Wightman (Converse/Reebok) as they scouted trends from actual teenagers on actual streets — a human-scouting model that the WGSN-style data platform has since industrialised ([The New Yorker, March 17, 1997](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1997/03/17/the-coolhunt); [Wiley reference entry on Cool Hunting](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/9781405165518.wbeos0998)).

**Why this matters for grassroots football.** The colours and graphic languages available to an amateur club — the teamwear catalogues, the sublimation patterns, the "new season" palettes — are downstream of forecasts sold years earlier to the sportswear giants. When five county-league clubs independently "choose" teal-and-graphite this season, that convergence was partly scheduled in a London office in 2024. A custom-kit studio that documents this machinery honestly can offer clubs a genuine alternative: design from club identity outward, rather than forecast inward.

**Article angles.**
1. "The people who decide what your kit will look like in 2028": an explainer on WGSN, colour forecasting and the 18-month pipeline — traced specifically through sportswear and teamwear.
2. From coolhunters to TrendCurve AI: a 30-year history of the trend industry, from Gladwell's 1997 street scouts to a machine that claims 94% accuracy — and what got lost.
3. The self-fulfilling forecast: why every brand's "bold new direction" looks the same, and what that homogenisation means for clubs that want to look like themselves.
4. Reverse-engineering a teamwear catalogue: matching this season's amateur kit palettes against forecast decks published two years earlier.

**Commercial bridge.** *Design Culture* — MOMUTO as the observer that explains the forecasting machine; secondary *Studio Notes*, showing how the studio consciously decides when to follow the forecast and when to design against it.

## 3. The Nostalgia Pendulum: Why the 90s Kit Came Back on Schedule

**Core insight.** Design revivals are not random: nostalgia returns on a roughly 20–30-year clock — first formalised by fashion historian James Laver in 1937 — because the people who consumed a culture as children become the people who produce culture as adults, and the retro football shirt boom landed precisely on schedule.

**Supporting evidence.**
- James Laver's *Taste and Fashion* (1937) proposed "Laver's Law," a timeline of how the same garment is perceived over time: "indecent" 10 years before its time, "daring" 1 year before, "smart" in season, "dowdy" 1 year after, "hideous" 10 years after, "ridiculous" 20 years after — then climbing back through "amusing" (30 years), "quaint" (50), "charming" (70), "romantic" (100) ([James Laver, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Laver); [Iowa State University re-evaluation of Laver's Law](https://dr.lib.iastate.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/c4945bd3-69a2-4cd6-b53a-d9ba2c1470d2/content); [Signal v. Noise summary](https://signalvnoise.com/posts/2474-lavers-law-of-fashion)).
- Patrick Metzger's "The Nostalgia Pendulum" (*The Patterning*, 2017), based on analysis of 500+ films and their remakes, found a rolling ~30-year cycle: creators revive the culture of their childhood, and because their fashionable peers grew up in the same period, "a kind of feedback loop" forms in which everyone contributes to reviving the same zeitgeist ([The Patterning, 2017](https://thepatterning.com/2017/02/13/the-nostalgia-pendulum-a-rolling-30-year-cycle-of-pop-culture-trends/)).
- The football application arrived on schedule: the reissue of iconic 1980s–2000s jerseys became a mass trend led by adidas, with Nike — historically reluctant — finally launching 1-to-1 remakes in 2025, and 2026 setting new records for retro releases; meanwhile authentic vintage prices rose *higher* as the market split into "accessible wearers" and "historical collectors" ([Footy Headlines, "Are Endless Remakes Killing the Retro Kit Market?", Feb 2026](https://www.footyheadlines.com/2026/02/remake-trend.html); [Classic Football Shirts reissues](https://www.classicfootballshirts.com/reissues)).
- "Blokecore" demonstrates the pendulum's timing with comic precision: American TikToker Brandon Huntley coined the term in late 2021 as a joke about British football YouTubers; his video went viral in April 2022, the hashtag passed ~94M views within months, Bella Hadid wore vintage shirts, Balenciaga put kits on the runway — and the aesthetic being revived was 80s/90s terrace dress, i.e. culture from exactly 25–30 years earlier ([Vice, "The Rise of 'Blokecore'"](https://www.vice.com/en/article/what-is-blokecore-tiktok-fashion-trend/); [MR PORTER Journal, 2022](https://www.mrporter.com/en-hk/journal/fashion/blokecore-british-football-style-oasis-blur-summer-2022-10715118); [YPulse, May 2022](https://www.ypulse.com/newsfeed/2022/05/25/the-latest-viral-tiktok-fashion-trend-blokecore-takes-its-inspiration-from-british-football-fans-from-the-80s-and-90s/)).

**Why this matters for grassroots football.** The people running amateur clubs — ordering kits, chairing committees — are largely 30- to 50-year-olds whose formative football memories are the very kits now cycling back into "amusing/quaint" territory on Laver's timeline. Nostalgia isn't a marketing gimmick at grassroots level; it's the actual emotional content of a club kit decision. A club choosing a 90s-style collar in 2026 is executing the pendulum, whether it knows it or not — and the pendulum also predicts what comes next (early-2000s techy minimalism, then 2010s template-era revivals).

**Article angles.**
1. "Right on schedule": plotting iconic kit revivals against Laver's Law and Metzger's pendulum — did the 90s shirt boom arrive exactly when the theory says it should? (Yes — show the math.)
2. The joke that became a market: how "blokecore" went from one American's TikTok gag to Balenciaga runways in under a year — a complete trend life-cycle case study football happens to own.
3. "Hideous, then holy": why the kits everyone mocked in 2005 (think shiny templates, garish gradients) are the collectibles of the 2020s — and which currently "hideous" kits are next.
4. The two retro markets: why mass reissues and rising vintage prices are the *same* nostalgia splitting into wearers and collectors — and where an amateur club's "future classic" fits.

**Commercial bridge.** *Design Culture* (the pendulum explained) with a strong *Ready to Play Thinking* tail — the practical question "do you want a kit that's fashionable now, or one that will be loved in 20 years?" is a nostalgia-pendulum question.

## 4. Direction of Flow: Trickle-Down, Bubble-Up, and Football's Terraces as a Trend Engine

**Core insight.** Trend theory spent a century arguing over whether style flows down from elites (Simmel, 1904) or bubbles up from the street (Blumberg, Polhemus, Hebdige) — and football fan culture, from the casuals to blokecore, is one of the clearest documented cases of the street winning.

**Supporting evidence.**
- Georg Simmel's "Fashion" (1904) formalised trickle-down theory: elites adopt styles to differentiate themselves, lower classes imitate, elites move on — a perpetual chase that generates fashion change ([Trickle-down fashion, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trickle-down_fashion)); Veblen's conspicuous consumption (1899) supplied the status engine ([Pressbooks, "Fashion Theories"](https://dressappearancediversity.pressbooks.sunycreate.cloud/chapter/fashion-theories/)).
- Paul Blumberg described the reverse flow in the 1970s, and anthropologist Ted Polhemus named it "bubble-up" in *Streetstyle* (1994): "styles which start life on the street corner have a way of ending up on the backs of top models on the world's most prestigious fashion catwalks" ([Trickle-up fashion, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trickle-up_fashion)).
- Dick Hebdige's *Subculture: The Meaning of Style* (1979) supplied the mechanism and the tragedy: subcultures create style to signal resistance and identity; media attention legitimises the style for mass audiences; commerce then "incorporates" it, converting subcultural signs into mass-produced commodities and defusing their meaning ([Pressbooks, "Fashion Theories"](https://dressappearancediversity.pressbooks.sunycreate.cloud/chapter/fashion-theories/)).
- Football is a canonical case: the casuals subculture of the late 1970s–80s — match-going supporters mixing football shirts, terrace sportswear and European designer labels — is precisely the street style that resurfaced four decades later as blokecore on TikTok and then on luxury runways (Balenciaga kits, Bella Hadid in vintage shirts), completing the full Hebdige arc from terrace resistance to catwalk commodity ([MR PORTER Journal](https://www.mrporter.com/en-hk/journal/fashion/blokecore-british-football-style-oasis-blur-summer-2022-10715118); [Vice](https://www.vice.com/en/article/what-is-blokecore-tiktok-fashion-trend/); [HBX Journal, 2024](https://hbx.com/journal/2024/05/blokecore-where-fashion-fuses-with-football)).

**Why this matters for grassroots football.** Amateur football is the street corner in Polhemus's sentence. The authentic material that luxury fashion keeps strip-mining — muddy pitches, clubhouse crests, hand-me-down shirts, local sponsor logos — is produced at grassroots level, for free, every weekend. Grassroots clubs are simultaneously the *origin* of the aesthetic (bubble-up) and the *last stop* of its commercialised echo (trickle-down): a Sunday club today can buy a teamwear kit "inspired by" a runway look that was itself inspired by Sunday clubs. Documenting that loop honestly is an editorial goldmine.

**Article angles.**
1. "The style loop": tracing one aesthetic full circle — terrace casuals (1980) → archive collectors (2000s) → TikTok blokecore (2022) → luxury runway (2023) → teamwear catalogue (2026) → back on a Sunday-league pitch.
2. Simmel vs Polhemus, settled by football: a readable history of the century-long trickle-down/bubble-up debate, using kits and terraces as the evidence base.
3. "Incorporation": Hebdige's warning applied to football — what happens to a fan culture's style when fashion strips it for parts, and what authenticity is left for the clubs that never stopped wearing it.
4. Why luxury fashion keeps coming back to football (and never the reverse): football as the last mass source of unmanufactured authenticity.

**Commercial bridge.** *Football Reality* — grassroots clubs as the true authors of the aesthetic the industry sells back to them; *Studio Notes* as the secondary pillar when MOMUTO shows how it designs from terrace culture rather than from its runway echo.

## 5. How Trends Die: Speed Kills, and the Algorithm Made Everything Faster

**Core insight.** Trends die by rule, not by accident: cultural tastes that are adopted quickly are abandoned quickly (proven across 100+ years of naming data), successful designs survive by being "most advanced yet acceptable," and TikTok's algorithm has compressed the trend life-cycle from decades to weeks — collapsing the very cycle the rest of this dossier describes.

**Supporting evidence.**
- Jonah Berger (Wharton) and Gaël Le Mens (UPF) analysed over a century of first-name adoption data in France and the US and found that names adopted faster die faster — and that parents actively *avoid* names rising too sharply because they read sudden popularity as a short-lived fad with negative symbolic value ("How adoption speed affects the abandonment of cultural tastes," *PNAS* 106, 2009, pp. 8146–8150: [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0812647106); [UPF summary](https://thescienceofbusiness.bsm.upf.edu/how-adoption-speed-affects-the-abandonment-of-cultural-tastes/)).
- Industrial designer Raymond Loewy's MAYA principle — "Most Advanced Yet Acceptable" — holds that audiences are torn between neophilia and neophobia, so the designs that endure push exactly as far as familiarity allows and no further; Derek Thompson's *Hit Makers* (2017) rebuilt this into a general theory of popularity: hits fuse novelty with familiarity ([IxDF on MAYA](https://ixdf.org/literature/topics/maya-principle); [Hit Makers, Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/30901608-hit-makers)).
- The acceleration: TikTok microtrends ("coastal grandma," "mob wife," etc.) now run a full trend cycle in months or weeks; commentary widely credits the algorithm — which rewards speed, novelty and virality "with a complete disregard for longevity" — with making the 20-year cycle "virtually obsolete" and driving overconsumption and waste ([NewsNation, "Is the 2000s retro? How social media is killing the trend cycle"](https://www.newsnationnow.com/business/tech/social-media-trend-cycle/); [The Mich, "TikTok's Rise of Microtrends," 2024](https://themich.org/2024/04/16/tiktoks-rise-of-microtrends/); [NPR, 2022](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/1116218375)).
- The deeper frame: Richard Dawkins coined "meme" in *The Selfish Gene* (1976) for a unit of culture that spreads by imitation and competes for attention — a lens under which a kit design is a meme whose fitness depends on visibility, copyability and identity value; Berger & Le Mens's finding is essentially a law of memetic mortality.
- Counter-signal already visible: "the microtrend has become a low-status behaviour" — trend-literate consumers are turning against visible fad-chasing, which Berger & Le Mens's identity-signaling mechanism predicts exactly ([TikTok, @eugbrandstrat brand-strategy commentary, 2025](https://www.tiktok.com/@eugbrandstrat/video/7472556002798341384)).

**Why this matters for grassroots football.** A grassroots kit has a mandatory life-span — clubs keep kits for two to five seasons because replacing them costs real money. That makes amateur clubs structurally immune to microtrends and structurally punished for adopting anything rising too fast: buy the viral aesthetic and, per Berger & Le Mens, you're buying the thing most likely to look dated before the kit wears out. The MAYA principle is arguably *the* design brief for amateur teamwear: advanced enough to feel new on presentation night, acceptable enough to survive five seasons of Laver's "dowdy" phase. Slow trends are a grassroots club's best friend.

**Article angles.**
1. "The five-season test": using Berger & Le Mens's adoption-speed law to build a practical rule for kit design — why the fastest-rising aesthetic is precisely the one an amateur club should avoid.
2. MAYA on grass: Raymond Loewy's principle rewritten as a teamwear design brief — how far can a club kit push before the committee (and the veterans' team) says no, and why that resistance is a feature.
3. "Your kit is a meme": a Dawkins-eye view of why some kit designs replicate across leagues and decades while objectively better ones vanish.
4. The algorithm vs. the kit cycle: what happens when trend life-spans (weeks) become shorter than kit procurement cycles (years) — and why that mismatch quietly protects football's visual culture from microtrend churn.

**Commercial bridge.** *Ready to Play Thinking* — durability-aware design guidance is a direct service argument; secondary *Studio Notes* on how the studio stress-tests designs against a five-season horizon rather than a TikTok cycle.
