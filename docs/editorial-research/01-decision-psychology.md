# Decision Psychology: Why Football Clubs Delay Jersey Decisions

**Abstract.** Fifty years of decision science converges on an uncomfortable truth: the slowest part of almost any project is not the doing, it is the deciding. This dossier assembles the strongest evidence — Simon's bounded rationality, the choice-overload literature and its replication debate, the planning fallacy and Flyvbjerg's overrun data, coordination-cost research on groups, the contested science of decision fatigue, and choice-architecture work on defaults and deadlines — to explain a pattern MOMUTO sees constantly in grassroots football: a club that can produce twenty kit ideas in one WhatsApp evening then takes three months to pick one. The literature says this is not a character flaw of amateur clubs. It is the predictable output of a specific decision environment: high preference uncertainty, no default, no deadline, a volunteer committee, and a decision that feels identity-laden (a crest, a colour) rather than technical. Crucially, the research also says the fixes are structural, not motivational — fewer well-differentiated options, a credible default, an external deadline, and outside-view time estimates. Every section below flags where the science is solid and where it has wobbled under replication, because our editorial credibility depends on reporting the debate, not the myth.

## Topic 1 — Choice overload is real, but only under conditions grassroots kit decisions meet almost perfectly

**Core insight.** More options do not reliably paralyze people — but they reliably do when options are complex, preferences are uncertain, and no option is obviously dominant, which is a precise description of choosing a custom jersey design by committee.

**Supporting evidence.**
- The canonical demonstration: Iyengar & Lepper (2000), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, the "jam study." A supermarket tasting booth with 24 jams attracted more browsers than one with 6, but conversion collapsed: roughly 30% of limited-choice shoppers bought jam versus about 3% in the extensive-choice condition. Barry Schwartz built *The Paradox of Choice* (2004) partly on this result. (Overview of the study and its afterlife: https://atticusli.com/replication-crisis/choice-overload-jam-study/)
- The correction: Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd (2010), "Can There Ever Be Too Many Options? A Meta-Analytic Review of Choice Overload," *Journal of Consumer Research* — across 50 experiments the mean effect of assortment size was virtually zero. Choice overload is not a universal law. (PDF: https://scheibehenne.com/ScheibehenneGreifenederTodd2010.pdf)
- The synthesis: Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman (2015), "Choice Overload: A Conceptual Review and Meta-Analysis," *Journal of Consumer Psychology*, 99 observations, N = 7,202. Choice overload reliably appears under four moderators: **choice set complexity, decision task difficulty, preference uncertainty, and decision goal**. When these are high, more options genuinely hurt. (PDF: https://chernev.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/ChoiceOverload_JCP_2015.pdf; journal page: https://myscp.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002)
- Ongoing methodological work continues to refine when overload binds, e.g. "A Better Test of Choice Overload" (arXiv, 2022): https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.03931

**Why this matters for grassroots football.** Score a kit decision against Chernev's four moderators. Complexity: every design differs on many attributes at once (collar, pattern, colourway, trim, typography). Task difficulty: the decision is made by several people, asynchronously, on phones. Preference uncertainty: most club officials have never articulated design preferences before; they discover them mid-argument. Decision goal: they are choosing *for keeps*, not browsing. Grassroots kit selection is close to a laboratory-perfect choice-overload environment — which predicts exactly the observed behaviour: enthusiasm at the browsing stage, paralysis at the commitment stage.

**Article angles.**
- "The Jam Study Comes to the Clubhouse" — the famous experiment, its replication crisis, and why the honest version of the science (overload is conditional) describes kit committees better than the pop version.
- "Twenty Mockups, Zero Orders" — why sending a club more design options often delays the order, told through the four moderators.
- "The Difference Between Browsing and Choosing" — 60% of shoppers stopped at the big jam table; 3% bought. Clubs love browsing designs; the psychology of why browsing is not deciding.

**Commercial bridge.** *Ready to Play Thinking.* A wall of finished, well-differentiated designs is choice architecture that attacks all four overload moderators: finished designs reduce complexity (you judge a whole, not attributes), curation reduces set size, and "ready" reframes the goal from open-ended creation to selection. The connection is inevitable because Ready to Play *is* a choice-set intervention — the editorial only has to explain the science honestly and the product logic follows.

## Topic 2 — Satisficing: the decision strategy that finishes, and the maximizer's tax

**Core insight.** Herbert Simon showed that real decision-makers cannot evaluate all options and should stop at "good enough"; Schwartz showed that people who refuse to — maximizers — end up slower, more regretful, and less happy with objectively similar outcomes.

**Supporting evidence.**
- Herbert Simon introduced **bounded rationality** in *Administrative Behavior* (1947) and coined **satisficing** in 1956: because of limits on attention, memory, information and time, agents search until an option clears an aspiration threshold, then stop. This work underpinned his 1978 Nobel Prize. (Nobel lecture: https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/simon-lecture.pdf; Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy overview: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/bounded-rationality/; Simon's contribution to public-organization decision-making: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/puar.13540)
- Schwartz, Ward, Monterosso, Lyubomirsky, White & Lehman (2002), "Maximizing Versus Satisficing: Happiness Is a Matter of Choice," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*. Across seven samples, maximizing correlated negatively with happiness, optimism, self-esteem and life satisfaction, and positively with depression, perfectionism and regret; maximizers were less satisfied with consumer decisions and more prone to social comparison. (PDF: https://bschwartz.domains.swarthmore.edu/maximizing.pdf; repository record: https://works.swarthmore.edu/fac-psychology/101/)
- Simon's key theoretical move — that a theory of choice must include a theory of *search* and of when to stop searching — is exactly the piece pop treatments of "analysis paralysis" omit. (https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/bounded-rationality/)

**Why this matters for grassroots football.** A kit committee has no natural stopping rule. Nobody defined what "good enough" looks like before browsing began, so every new mockup re-opens the search. Worse, the loudest committee member is often the club's maximizer — the person who "just wants to see one more version." Simon's insight reframes the problem: the club doesn't need better taste or more options; it needs an aspiration level set in advance ("club colours, budget X, ready by pre-season") and permission to stop when a design clears it. Schwartz's data adds the emotional kicker: the club that agonizes longest will probably *also* be less happy with the shirt it finally picks.

**Article angles.**
- "The Nobel Prize for Knowing When to Stop" — Simon's satisficing, told as the antidote to the club design chat that never ends.
- "Every Club Has a Maximizer" — a recognizable committee character piece grounded in the Schwartz maximization scale.
- "Define 'Done' Before You Open the Catalogue" — practical piece on aspiration levels as the missing tool in kit decisions.

**Commercial bridge.** *Ready to Play Thinking*, with a secondary feed into *Studio Notes*. "Ready to Play" is institutionalized satisficing: the design work has been finished to a professional aspiration level so the club's only job is the stop-decision. Studio Notes can honestly document how MOMUTO's designers set their own stopping rules — showing that "finished" is itself a design discipline.

## Topic 3 — The planning fallacy: clubs are the last optimists about their own timeline

**Core insight.** People systematically predict best-case timelines for their own projects even when they know base rates say otherwise — so a club that "has plenty of time before the season" is making the same forecasting error as the builders of the Sydney Opera House, at smaller scale.

**Supporting evidence.**
- Kahneman & Tversky named the **planning fallacy** in 1979: forecasters take an "inside view" of the singular case and neglect distributional evidence from similar past projects. (Overview with origins: https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/planning-fallacy; Buehler, Griffin & Peetz review, "The Planning Fallacy: Cognitive, Motivational, and Social Origins": https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0065260110430014)
- Buehler, Griffin & Ross (1994), "Exploring the planning fallacy: Why people underestimate their task completion times," *JPSP*: honours students estimated 33.9 days to finish their theses; actual completion averaged roughly 56 days, and most missed even their self-described "worst-case" estimates. (PDF: https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/biases/67_J_Personality_and_Social_Psychology_366,_1994.pdf)
- The Sydney Opera House: estimated at 4 years and AU$7m; delivered in 14 years at over AU$100m — the textbook monument to the inside view. (https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/planning-fallacy)
- Flyvbjerg, Holm & Buhl (2002) found cost overruns in ~9 of 10 transport infrastructure projects, averaging 45% (rail), 34% (fixed links), 20% (roads); the Oxford Olympics Study (Flyvbjerg et al., 2016) found every Olympic Games since 1960 overran, averaging 156–157% in real terms. Kahneman called Flyvbjerg's remedy — **reference class forecasting**, budgeting from the actual outcomes of similar past projects — "the single most important piece of advice regarding how to increase accuracy in forecasting." (Oxford Olympics Study: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1607.04484; overrun prevalence: https://www.academia.edu/20497640/How_common_and_how_large_are_cost_overruns_in_transport_infrastructure_projects; reference class forecasting review: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09537287.2025.2578708)
- Hofstadter's Law, coined in *Gödel, Escher, Bach* (1979): "It always takes longer than you expect, even when you take into account Hofstadter's Law." Hofstadter formulated it watching chess-computer predictions slip for decades — and his own book famously overran its schedule. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hofstadter%27s_law)

**Why this matters for grassroots football.** The kit timeline has two halves: a fixed, known half (MOMUTO production 7–12 days, shipping 10–15; 25–30 days door to door) and an unbounded, unknown half (the club deciding). Clubs apply the inside view to the second half — "we'll agree on a design next week" — exactly as thesis students did, and with the same result: the *decision* consumes the schedule slack, and then the fixed logistics arithmetic makes lateness certain. Reference class forecasting translated to grassroots scale is one question: "How long did the kit decision take last time — for you, or for any club you know?" The honest answer (weeks to months) is the base rate clubs refuse to apply to themselves.

**Article angles.**
- "The Slowest Part of Ordering Jerseys Isn't Manufacturing" — the flagship piece: production time is a known constant; decision time is the unmodeled variable.
- "Your Club Is a Tiny Sydney Opera House" — the planning fallacy from megaprojects to matchday, with Flyvbjerg's numbers.
- "Hofstadter's Law, Pre-Season Edition" — why 'plenty of time' in June becomes panic in August, every single year.
- "Ask a Different Question: How Long Did It Take Last Time?" — reference class forecasting as a one-line tool for club secretaries.

**Commercial bridge.** *Football Reality.* This is documentary territory: the recurring, dateable, seasonal ritual of clubs starting kit conversations too late. The connection to MOMUTO is inevitable without being promotional — 25–30 days door to door is simply the fixed term in the equation; the article's subject is the variable term nobody budgets for.

## Topic 4 — Committees, coordination costs, and the bike shed: why groups argue longest about what matters least

**Core insight.** Adding people to a decision adds coordination costs that routinely exceed the benefit of extra heads — and groups predictably spend their time on the trivial, graspable parts of a decision (Parkinson's "bike shed") because everyone feels entitled to an opinion about them.

**Supporting evidence.**
- Straub, Tsvetkova & Yasseri (2023), "The cost of coordination can exceed the benefit of collaboration in performing complex tasks," *Collective Intelligence*: in dyads performing a complex problem-solving task, the cost of coordinating consistently outweighed the leverage of having a partner; on complex tasks, individuals could outperform pairs. (https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/26339137231156912)
- The small-group communication literature's settled finding: groups can be more accurate and generate more ideas, but are inherently slower and costlier than individuals because discussion, alignment and disagreement consume time from every member. (https://courses.lumenlearning.com/wm-principlesofmanagement/chapter/barriers-to-individual-decision-making/; https://pressbooks.pub/smallgroup/chapter/decision-making-in-groups/)
- C. Northcote Parkinson (1957), *Parkinson's Law, or the Pursuit of Progress* (seeded by his satirical Economist essay of 19 November 1955): the **Law of Triviality**. His fictional finance committee approves a £10 million nuclear reactor in two and a half minutes — nobody understands it, so nobody objects — then debates a £350 bicycle shed for forty-five minutes, because everyone can picture a bicycle shed. Also the umbrella law: "work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion." (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_triviality)
- Governance research on volunteer sports clubs documents the same dynamics in football's real committees: overload and burnout risk among volunteer boards, operational delays tied to volunteer turnover, and clubs deliberately reforming meetings to be "shorter, more decisive and outcomes oriented." (Sports Governance Academy, "Organisational governance & grassroots sports clubs: the 7Fs Framework": https://www.sportsgovernanceacademy.org.uk/media/mlcp4gbu/sga-organisational-governance-grassroots-def-final-full-0411.pdf; The Football Collective, written evidence to the UK Parliament: https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/7252/html/; sport volunteering governance paradox: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2026.1746196/full)

**Why this matters for grassroots football.** A jersey is the perfect bike shed. It is the most *graspable* item on any club agenda — everyone has an opinion on navy versus royal blue, nobody defers to expertise, and unlike the boiler repair or the insurance renewal, disagreeing feels like participation rather than obstruction. Meanwhile the decision has no owner: kit typically belongs to "the committee," i.e., to no one. Parkinson predicts the observed pathology precisely — the £350-shirt debate outlasting the £10,000-budget approval — and the coordination-cost research explains why adding "let's ask the players too" makes it slower still. The editorial opportunity is affection, not mockery: the arguing *is* the belonging. But belonging needs a deadline.

**Article angles.**
- "The Bike Shed Is a Football Shirt" — Parkinson's 1957 committee transplanted to a Tuesday-night clubhouse meeting, almost line for line.
- "Nine People, One Shirt, Three Months" — coordination cost as arithmetic: what each added voice actually costs in calendar time.
- "Give the Kit a Minister" — the case for a single accountable kit decision-owner with a consultation window, drawn from governance reform literature on volunteer boards.
- "The Meeting Is the Point (Until It Isn't)" — a sympathetic essay on why clubs argue about shirts: because it's the one agenda item that feels like identity.

**Commercial bridge.** *Football Reality* primarily; *Design Culture* secondarily. This is reported, observational content about how clubs actually govern themselves — parliamentary evidence and governance frameworks give it non-promotional spine. The bridge to MOMUTO is structural: a finished-design wall converts an unbounded generative debate ("what should our kit be?") into a bounded selection vote ("A, B or C by Friday?") — the exact meeting-reform move the governance literature recommends.

## Topic 5 — Decision fatigue: a seductive story the evidence only partly supports

**Core insight.** The popular claim that willpower drains like a battery has largely failed replication — but the weaker, defensible claim survives: long unstructured decision sessions degrade in quality toward the end, which is enough to indict the 11pm WhatsApp kit vote.

**Supporting evidence.**
- The origin: Baumeister's ego-depletion program (1990s–2000s) held that self-control and decision-making draw on one finite resource. (Review of the theory and its criticisms: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/350832721_Ego_depletion_A_review_of_criticisms_along_with_new_perspectives_for_the_replicable_investigation_of_self-control_fatigue_as_a_multicomponent_phenomenon)
- The famous field study: Danziger, Levav & Avnaim-Pesso (2011, *PNAS*) analyzed over 1,100 Israeli parole rulings; favorable rulings fell from ~65% at session start toward ~0% before food breaks, snapping back to ~65% after. Widely retold ("hungry judges"), it has been seriously challenged: case-ordering artifacts (unrepresented prisoners scheduled late) and simulations suggesting the effect size is too large to be plausible as pure fatigue. (Critical overview: https://grokipedia.com/page/Decision_fatigue)
- The reckoning: Hagger et al. (2016), a pre-registered Registered Replication Report across 23 labs and 2,000+ participants, found no reliable ego-depletion effect; Baumeister's rebuttal argued the protocol was too weak to induce depletion. (Commentary on the RRR and Baumeister's response: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5329006/)
- Current position: critical reviews treat decision fatigue as real but multi-component and context-dependent — mental fatigue, time-on-task and choice repetition degrade decisions, without requiring the "resource" metaphor. (Critical review of decision fatigue and choice overload: http://www.jpub.org/journal-admin/uploads/articles/hi515.pdf)

**Why this matters for grassroots football.** Two lessons, one editorial and one practical. Editorial: MOMUTO should *never* retail the hungry-judges anecdote uncritically — reporting the replication fight is exactly what separates The Athletic register from content-marketing register, and the ego-depletion saga is genuinely one of psychology's best reform stories. Practical: the surviving science still says decision quality is worst at the end of long sessions — and grassroots kit decisions habitually happen at the end of everything: last agenda item of a two-hour meeting, last message of a 200-message group chat, late at night after work. The defensible advice is scheduling, not willpower: decide first, not last; in a short dedicated session, not the dregs of a long one.

**Article angles.**
- "The Hungry Judges and the Midnight Kit Vote" — tell the famous study *and* its debunking, then land on what actually survives scrutiny.
- "Last Item on the Agenda" — why the kit decision always gets the worst ten minutes of the meeting, and what happens if you give it the first ten instead.
- "Psychology's Best Failure" — the ego-depletion replication saga as a story about updating beliefs, mirrored onto clubs updating how they decide.

**Commercial bridge.** *Studio Notes / Football Reality.* This topic's value is credibility: an essay that debunks a famous pop-psych claim while extracting the usable residue positions MOMUTO's editorial as trustworthy. The practical residue ("give the decision your best hour, not your last") feeds Football Reality service pieces without touching the product at all — which is precisely why readers will trust the pieces that do.

## Topic 6 — Choice architecture: defaults and deadlines finish decisions that willpower never will

**Core insight.** The largest documented swings in human decision behaviour come not from persuasion but from restructuring the choice — a default that stands unless overridden, and an external deadline that converts intention into action.

**Supporting evidence.**
- Johnson & Goldstein (2003), "Do Defaults Save Lives?", *Science*: organ-donor consent in opt-in European countries ran roughly 4–27%, versus 86–99.98% in opt-out countries — Germany (opt-in) ~12% beside Austria (opt-out) 99.98% — with the default explaining almost the entire gap. (Follow-up paper: https://www.dangoldstein.com/papers/JohnsonGoldstein_Defaults_Transplantation2004.pdf; original record: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/8996952_Do_Defaults_Save_Lives)
- Thaler & Sunstein, *Nudge* (2008), generalized this into **choice architecture**: there is no neutral way to present options; defaults, ordering and framing always shape outcomes, so the only question is whether they are designed deliberately. (Context: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8349348/)
- Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002), "Procrastination, Deadlines, and Performance: Self-Control by Precommitment," *Psychological Science*: students *chose* to self-impose costly deadlines to fight procrastination, and deadlines improved performance — but self-set deadlines were set suboptimally; evenly spaced, externally imposed deadlines produced the best outcomes. (PDF: https://web.mit.edu/ariely/www/MIT/Papers/deadlines.pdf; journal record: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1467-9280.00441)
- Parkinson's umbrella law — work expands to fill the time available (The Economist, 19 Nov 1955; book 1957) — is the folk statement of why open-ended decision windows never close on their own. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_triviality)

**Why this matters for grassroots football.** The typical club kit process has the worst possible architecture: no default (a blank canvas), no deadline (until the fixture list imposes a brutal one), and a committee (Topic 4). The science suggests the same club with the same people decides in days if the architecture changes: a concrete default design ("this is what we order unless someone proposes better by the 15th"), an external decision deadline set backwards from the season start minus the known 25–30-day logistics window, and a named owner. None of this requires anyone to become more decisive — that is the whole point of the Johnson & Goldstein result: Austrians are not 88 points more altruistic than Germans; their form was designed differently. Clubs are not indecisive; their kit process is undesigned.

**Article angles.**
- "Austria, Germany, and Your Club's New Away Shirt" — the most startling chart in behavioural science, and what a 'default kit' would do for a committee.
- "Deadlines Are a Technology" — Ariely's students paid real costs to bind their own hands; a piece on why clubs should set their kit deadline in June, in writing, in public.
- "Count Backwards from Kickoff" — the season start date minus shipping minus production equals the true decision deadline; a service piece that makes the invisible deadline visible.
- "Nobody Decides on a Blank Page" — why 'send us your ideas' is the slowest brief in football, and what a strong default does to a group chat.

**Commercial bridge.** *Ready to Play Thinking* — this is its intellectual foundation. Ready to Play is, literally, a default: a finished design that can be ordered as-is or customized, converting the club's task from creation (unbounded) to override (bounded). The Johnson & Goldstein framing makes the bridge inevitable and non-promotional: the article is about the power of defaults; the product is simply a default, well designed. The backwards-from-kickoff arithmetic also feeds *Football Reality* seasonal service content.
